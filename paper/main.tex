\begin{abstract}
Conditional flow matching (CFM) trained to convergence on a finite dataset is often
described as ``memorising'' the training data: overtraining is reported to collapse
the conditional generative law onto individual training examples. We give an exact,
population-level account of why this happens. Fixing the dataset and the
deterministic linear interpolant, the $L^2$-optimal velocity field is a
posterior-weighted average of per-example collapsed fields, and under hard
conditioning with identifying labels this average degenerates to a single atom: the
exact minimiser has \emph{zero} conditional variance and zero loss
(Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}). We then show that the standard remedy---label
smoothing, i.e.\ adding Gaussian noise to the conditioning variable---turns this hard
selection into \emph{exact kernel regression over the training atoms}: the generated
endpoint law becomes a Nadaraya--Watson mixture $\sum_i p_i^{(h)}(y)\,\delta_{x^i}$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}) whose moments are a parameter-free reference curve
computable directly from the training set (Proposition~\ref{prop:moments}). This
remedy, however, can only match moments; it cannot recover the posterior, since the
generated law remains atomic at every bandwidth $h$, yielding an $h$-independent
Wasserstein floor (Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity}). That obstruction names its own
cure---move the support---and smoothing the \emph{endpoint} does so, restoring an
absolutely continuous law (Proposition~\ref{prop:tgtnoise}); measuring the floor
directly shows it is small at our default configuration, correcting an earlier reading
of our own MMD curves. Two superficially similar remedies are moreover not
interchangeable: label noise moves the population endpoint, interpolant noise provably
does not, at any noise level (Proposition~\ref{prop:prop17}). Across five seeds, a trained MLP tracks the kernel
reference to within seed noise ($\text{ratio-to-kernel}\approx1.00$ for $h\ge0.05$),
and we reproduce the same collapse on a Gaussian-mixture posterior, on MNIST and
CIFAR-10 inpainting, and under adversarially shuffled labels. At realistic capacity---a
$35.75$M-parameter DDPM U-Net on CIFAR-10, $d{=}3072$---hard-conditioning collapse is
unambiguous ($415\times$ in $\tr\Cov$; the generated mean identifies the correct
training image at every evaluation condition), while the smoothed prediction holds only
in direction: regressing measured on predicted variance across conditions gives a
log--log slope of $0.481$ where the theory predicts $1$, and the atomicity obstruction,
exact for the population minimiser, is violated by $38\%$ of samples at the widest
bandwidth we test.
Throughout, we distinguish carefully between exact population consequences and
empirical findings that the population theory does not, by itself, determine.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Flow matching and stochastic-interpolant models
\citep{lipman2023flow,albergo2023building,liu2023rectified,albergo2023stochastic}
are usually trained by regressing a velocity field against a linear path between a
noise sample and a data sample \citep{tong2024improving}. On a
finite dataset, exact minimisation of this objective against the \emph{empirical}
data law is known to induce memorisation: deployed diffusion models can be made to
reproduce individual training images verbatim
\citep{carlini2023extracting,somepalli2023diffusion}, and a recent line of work has
documented that conditional flow-matching models, trained past the point of good
posterior calibration, produce increasingly deterministic (low-variance) conditional
samples that coincide with individual training examples
\citep{physicscfm2026,gradvar2025}.

We give an exact, population-level account of this mechanism. Fixing the training
set $\D=\{(x^i,y^i)\}_{i=1}^N$ and treating only the interpolation randomness as
random, we characterise the exact population minimiser of the flow-matching
objective over all square-integrable vector fields, together with the endpoint law
of its flow.

Two established lines bracket this paper, and it is worth saying exactly which part of
the picture each already owns. On one side, that the empirical minimiser memorises is
well established
\citep{pidstrigach2022manifolds,gu2023memorization,kadkhodaie2024generalization,biroli2023diffusion,kamb2024creativity,buchanan2025edge};
\citet{scarvelis2025closedform} in particular already identify the closed-form
\emph{unconditional} denoiser on a finite training set as a kernel-weighted average of
training points that degenerates to a mixture of atoms, and
\citet{chen2026scoresmoothing} analyse smoothing the empirical score as the escape. Nearest to us on
this side is \citet{smola2026support}, who read the \emph{spatial} factor
$\pi_0(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t})$ of the index posterior as a Nadaraya--Watson smoother whose
bandwidth is the flow time $1-t$, sharpening to a nearest-neighbour rule as $t\to1$. That
is the other factor of the same product \eqref{eq:kernel-field}: our subject is the
\emph{label} factor $K_h(y-y^i)$, whose bandwidth is a modelling choice rather than a
consequence of the clock, and what we extract from it is the endpoint law and its moments
rather than the field. The two readings compose; neither implies the other. On
the other side, adding Gaussian noise to a conditioning signal is standard practice and
not something we propose: it is the conditioning augmentation of \citet{ho2022cascaded},
and \citet{sadat2024cads} anneal exactly such noise at inference \emph{for the express
purpose of restoring conditional diversity}---the empirical counterpart of the very
phenomenon analysed here.

What neither line supplies is the object connecting them. We show that the second is not
a heuristic patch on the first but is \emph{exactly} Nadaraya--Watson regression over the
training atoms (Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}). That identification does two things a purely
empirical account cannot. It replaces a tuning knob by a parameter-free reference curve
computable from the training set alone, against which a trained model can be audited
(Proposition~\ref{prop:moments}). And it exposes a ceiling the empirical literature does
not report: because the smoothed law remains supported on the training atoms at
\emph{every} bandwidth, conditioning noise can match moments but can never recover the
posterior, with a Wasserstein floor independent of $h$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity}). Closest in spirit is \citet{buchanan2025edge}, who characterise a
memorisation/generalisation \emph{phase transition} in \emph{unconditional}
diffusion models as denoiser capacity $M$ ranges from the number of modes $K$ up to
the dataset size $N$. That is a capacity-driven account of the same broad
phenomenon we study from the label/bandwidth side---their $M\colon K\to N$
interpolation is the capacity analogue of our $h\colon0\to\infty$ interpolation
(Corollaries~\ref{cor:zerobw} and~\ref{cor:infbw})---and neither result subsumes
the other. Our contribution is the \emph{conditional and kernel-regression
structure} underlying the collapse, which this literature does not isolate:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Conditional sharpening.} Hard conditioning turns full-empirical-measure
  memorisation into \emph{single-atom} memorisation---zero conditional variance
  (Corollary~\ref{cor:distinction}). The correct contrast with the unconditional
  model is not ``memorises vs.\ does not'' but \emph{which} empirical measure is
  memorised. This is not the ordinary finite-sample memorisation of an
  over-parameterised network fitting a fixed batch (Appendix~\ref{sec:theory-d2},
  adapted from \citealp{gradvar2025}, is exactly that phenomenon, for the
  \emph{unconditional} model): under hard conditioning $y^i$ identifies the training
  atom \emph{deterministically}, so the collapse is already present in the exact
  population minimiser, for every $x_0$ and every fresh draw---no finite batch, no
  fitting, no resampling escape route.
\item \textbf{Label smoothing $\equiv$ kernel regression.} Conditioning noise is an
  existing technique \citep{ho2022cascaded,sadat2024cads}; what is new is that it makes
  the population endpoint an \emph{exact} Nadaraya--Watson mixture
  \citep{nadaraya1964estimating,watson1964smooth} over training atoms
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}), turning a $0/1$ statement into a parameter-free
  reference curve $\Covh(y)$ a trained model can be audited against
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:moments}).
\item \textbf{Separation of two remedies.} Label noise changes the population
  endpoint; interpolant noise provably does not, at any noise level
  (Propositions~\ref{prop:prop17} and \ref{prop:factors}).
\item \textbf{Atomicity obstruction, and the escape from it.} The smoothed law is
  atomic for every $h$, yielding an $h$-independent Wasserstein floor
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity}). The obstruction names its own cure---move the
  support---and endpoint smoothing does exactly that
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:tgtnoise}). We \emph{measure} the floor rather than assert
  it, and find it small at our default $(d,N)$, correcting an earlier reading of our
  own MMD curves.
\item \textbf{Representation vs.\ optimisation.} A uniform Lipschitz bound gives a
  measurable loss floor $d/(3L)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}); measuring it shows the
  observed plateau is \emph{not} explained by that mechanism. Separately
  (Appendix~\ref{sec:theory-d2}) no finite network with globally Lipschitz
  activations represents the collapsed field's $1/(1-t)$ blow-up pointwise on
  $[0,1)$---a claim we keep explicitly distinct from a loss floor.
\end{enumerate}

In one line: \emph{conditional collapse is the zero-bandwidth limit of a
kernel-weighted empirical conditional flow.} On scope
(Section~\ref{sec:scope}): the theory characterises the exact population minimiser for
the empirical data law, not what a finite network trained by SGD attains, so we
separate quantities it determines from quantities it does not---the dependence of
observed collapse on $N$ or $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}$---which we report as empirical
findings, never as ``predictions.''

\section{Setup and notation}
\label{sec:setup}

Fix a training set $\D=\{(x^i,y^i)\}_{i=1}^N$ with $x^i\in\R^d$, $y^i\in\R^k$,
empirical joint law $\rhohat_{XY}=\frac1N\sum_i\delta_{(x^i,y^i)}$ and marginal
$\rhohat_X=\frac1N\sum_i\delta_{x^i}$. The training set is fixed (deterministic);
randomness comes only from
\[
I\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\dots,N\},\qquad X_0\sim\pi_0=\N(0,I_d),\qquad t\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1),
\]
drawn independently, together with label noise $\varepsilon\sim\N(0,I_k)$ when
$h>0$. Set $X_1=x^I$ and, for the deterministic linear interpolant,
\[
X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1,\qquad U:=\dot X_t=X_1-X_0.
\]
The smoothed label is $\widetilde Y=y^I+h\varepsilon$ with Gaussian kernel
$K_h(u)=(2\pi h^2)^{-k/2}\exp(-|u|^2/2h^2)$. The objective is
\begin{equation}
L_h(v)=\E\big[\,|U-v(X_t,t,\widetilde Y)|^2\,\big].
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
For $h=0$ we write $\widetilde Y=Y=y^I$ and $L_0$. Let $\bar x=\frac1N\sum_i x^i$ and
$\Xhat=\frac1N\sum_i(x^i-\bar x)(x^i-\bar x)^\top$. In the linear-Gaussian instance
used experimentally, $y=Ax+\eta$ with $\eta\sim\N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2 I_k)$,
which yields a Gaussian analytic posterior with covariance $\Sigmapost$.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\emph{Scope marker.} Sections~\ref{sec:theory-hard}--\ref{sec:theory-noise}
characterise the exact population minimiser over $L^2$-measurable vector fields for
the empirical data law, with $\D$ \emph{fixed}; Section~\ref{sec:theory-floor}
quantifies one reason a finite trained network differs. Two results are the
exception and change the probabilistic setting deliberately:
Propositions~\ref{prop:expansion} and~\ref{prop:nwrate} treat $\D$ as an i.i.d.\
sample and are asymptotic in $N$, because their content is precisely what happens as
the fixed-$N$ picture is left behind; we mark the switch where it occurs. We state
the load-bearing results here and defer all proofs to Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. The
projection identity underlying everything is the standard $L^2$ decomposition behind
every flow-matching objective \citep{lipman2023flow,albergo2023building,liu2023rectified};
we restate it because every later result is an evaluation of the conditional
expectation it identifies, and record no proof for it.

\begin{proposition}[$L^2$ projection]
\label{prop:projection}
For every square-integrable $v$,
$L_h(v)=\E[\Var(U\mid X_t,t,\widetilde Y)]+\E\,|v-\E[U\mid X_t,t,\widetilde Y]|^2$,
so the a.e.-unique minimiser is $\vstar(x,t,y)=\E[U\mid X_t=x,t,\widetilde Y=y]$ with
$\inf_v L_h=\E[\Var(U\mid X_t,t,\widetilde Y)]$.
\end{proposition}

Two facts make the conditional expectations legitimate and the flows well posed
without any additional assumption (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}): (i) for $t<1$ the map
$X_0\mapsto X_t=(1-t)X_0+tx^i$ is an invertible affine change of variables, with
$X_0=(X_t-tx^i)/(1-t)$ and conditional density
$p(x\mid I=i,t)=(1-t)^{-d}\pi_0\!\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)>0$ for every $x$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}); (ii) any field of the form
$v(x,t)=\sum_i w_i(x,t)\,(x^i-x)/(1-t)$ with smooth weights summing to one is
$C^\infty$ hence locally Lipschitz on $\R^d\times[0,1)$, so its ODE has a unique
solution up to $t=1$ and endpoint laws are the well-defined weak limits
$p_1=\lim_{t\uparrow1}p_t$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:wellposed}).

\subsection{Hard conditioning: exact collapse}
\label{sec:theory-hard}

We begin with the base case: a raw, unsmoothed label ($h=0$) that identifies its
training example uniquely. Proposition~\ref{prop:projection} already tells us the
exact minimiser is a conditional expectation; the question is what that conditional
expectation collapses to once the conditioning event pins down a single index $i$.

\begin{proposition}[Exact conditional collapse; $h=0$]
\label{prop:collapse}
Assume $y^1,\dots,y^N$ are pairwise distinct. Then
(a) $v_0^\star(x,t,y^i)=(x^i-x)/(1-t)$ for $t<1$;
(b) $\inf_v L_0(v)=0$;
(c) the ODE $\dot x_t=(x^i-x_t)/(1-t)$ has the explicit solution
$x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx^i$, so $x_t\to x^i$ for every $x_0$; and
(d) $p_1^{\mathrm{cond}}(\cdot\mid y^i)=\delta_{x^i}$, hence
$\Cov[p_1^{\mathrm{cond}}(\cdot\mid y^i)]=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) Distinctness makes $\{Y=y^i\}$ identify $I=i$, so $X_1=x^i$ is deterministic and,
by Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}, $X_0=(x-tx^i)/(1-t)$ is determined by $(x,t,i)$. Hence
$U=x^i-X_0=(x^i-x)/(1-t)$ is $\sigma(X_t,t,Y)$-measurable and equals its conditional
expectation. (b) $U-v_0^\star=0$ a.s. (c) Multiplying by $(1-t)$ gives
$\frac{d}{dt}\big[\frac{x_t-x^i}{1-t}\big]=0$, so $\frac{x_t-x^i}{1-t}\equiv x_0-x^i$.
(d) The endpoint is $x^i$ independently of $x_0$.
\end{proof}

If labels are not distinct, collapse is onto the label's empirical conditional
support: for $\mathcal I_y=\{i:y^i=y\}$, $p_1^{\mathrm{cond}}(\cdot\mid y)=
|\mathcal I_y|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_y}\delta_{x^i}$. Collapse is always to the
empirical conditional law, which is a single atom exactly when the label is an
identifier.

\begin{proposition}[Unconditional endpoint is the full empirical law]
\label{prop:uncond}
For $L_{\mathrm{unc}}(v)=\E|X_1-X_0-v(X_t,t)|^2$ and $t<1$, with
$q_i(x,t)=\Pr(I=i\mid X_t=x,t)\propto\pi_0\!\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)$,
\[
v_{\mathrm{unc}}^\star(x,t)=\sum_i q_i(x,t)\,\frac{x^i-x}{1-t},\qquad
p_1^{\mathrm{unc}}=\rhohat_X=\tfrac1N\sum_i\delta_{x^i}.
\]
Moreover $\inf_v L_{\mathrm{unc}}>0$ whenever $x^i\ne x^j$ for some $i\ne j$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[The correct conditional/unconditional distinction]
\label{cor:distinction}
Both exact population flows are supported on the training set:
$p_1^{\mathrm{cond}}(\cdot\mid y^i)=\delta_{x^i}$ and
$p_1^{\mathrm{unc}}=\frac1N\sum_i\delta_{x^i}$. The distinction is therefore not
``memorises vs.\ does not'' but \emph{single-example} vs.\
\emph{full-empirical-measure} memorisation, with observable difference in the
conditional second moment:
\begin{equation}
\Cov[p_1^{\mathrm{cond}}(\cdot\mid y^i)]=0
\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad
\Cov[p_1^{\mathrm{unc}}]=\Xhat.
\label{eq:distinction}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Both halves are immediate: the conditional statement is
Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}(d) and the unconditional one is
Proposition~\ref{prop:uncond}; the corollary only places them side by side, which is
the entire point.

The often-cited observation $\tr\Cov\approx\tr\Xhat$ for the unconditional baseline
is precisely \eqref{eq:distinction}, i.e.\ evidence of full-empirical-measure
memorisation, \emph{not} of non-memorisation.

Two empirical findings in the literature are, on this reading, the same statement at
scale. \citet{somepalli2023copying} report that verbatim replication ``often does not
happen for unconditional models, while it is common in the text-conditional case'' in
Stable Diffusion, and that randomising captions mitigates it: that asymmetry is
\eqref{eq:distinction}, and caption randomisation is label smoothing.
\citet{gu2023memorization} report that conditioning on \emph{uninformative random}
labels sharply increases memorisation, which is puzzling if one expects informative
conditioning to matter; Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} says informativeness is
irrelevant and identification is everything, and our adversarially shuffled-label
experiment (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) is that prediction tested directly.

\subsection{Label smoothing is exactly kernel regression}
\label{sec:theory-kernel}

The natural fix for a label that identifies its example too precisely is to blur it:
replace $y^i$ by $\widetilde Y=y^i+h\varepsilon$. This is not a heuristic---the
population minimiser becomes exactly the velocity field of a Nadaraya--Watson kernel
regression over the training atoms.

\begin{proposition}[Kernel-weighted population minimiser]
\label{prop:kernel-field}
For $h>0$, $t<1$,
\begin{equation}
\vstar(x,t,y)=\sum_i w_i^{(h)}(x,t,y)\,\frac{x^i-x}{1-t},\quad
w_i^{(h)}=\frac{\pi_0\!\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)K_h(y-y^i)}
{\sum_j\pi_0\!\big(\tfrac{x-tx^j}{1-t}\big)K_h(y-y^j)}.
\label{eq:kernel-field}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
Equation~\eqref{eq:kernel-field} factorises the index posterior into a spatial
source-consistency term $\pi_0(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t})$ and a label kernel $K_h(y-y^i)$.
Write $p_i^{(h)}(y):=K_h(y-y^i)/\sum_j K_h(y-y^j)$ for the normalised label weights.
Lemma~\ref{lem:mixture-coupling} (appendix) identifies $\vstar$ with the conditional
velocity of the coupling $I\sim p^{(h)}(y)$, $X_1=x^I$, which is what lets us read
off the endpoint law:

\begin{theorem}[Endpoint law under label smoothing]
\label{thm:endpoint}
For every $h>0$ and query $y$,
\begin{equation}
p_h^{\mathrm{gen}}(\cdot\mid y)=\sum_{i=1}^N p_i^{(h)}(y)\,\delta_{x^i}.
\label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{corollary}[Zero bandwidth at fixed $N$]
\label{cor:zerobw}
If $y$ has a unique nearest label $i^\star=\arg\min_i|y-y^i|$, then
$p_i^{(h)}(y)\to\mathbf 1\{i=i^\star\}$ as $h\to0$, so
$p_h^{\mathrm{gen}}(\cdot\mid y)\Rightarrow\delta_{x^{i^\star}}$; at $y=y^i$ this
recovers Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{corollary}[Infinite bandwidth at fixed $N$]
\label{cor:infbw}
$p_i^{(h)}(y)\to1/N$ for every $i$ as $h\to\infty$, so
$p_h^{\mathrm{gen}}(\cdot\mid y)\Rightarrow p_1^{\mathrm{unc}}$.
\end{corollary}
So $h$ interpolates continuously between the two hard regimes of
Section~\ref{sec:theory-hard}: $\delta_{x^{i^\star}}\to\sum_i
p_i^{(h)}(y)\delta_{x^i}\to\frac1N\sum_i\delta_{x^i}$ as $h$ grows from $0$ to
$\infty$.

\begin{proposition}[Exact finite-$N$ moments]
\label{prop:moments}
$\bar x_h(y)=\sum_i p_i^{(h)}(y)\,x^i$ and
$\Covh(y)=\sum_i p_i^{(h)}(y)\,(x^i-\bar x_h(y))(x^i-\bar x_h(y))^\top$, both exactly
computable from the training set for any $h$. They are the correct reference against
which generated moments must be compared. $\tr\Covh(y)$ need not be monotone in $h$;
monotonicity is not implied and must be reported empirically.
\end{proposition}

This is the mean and covariance of the discrete law $\sum_ip_i^{(h)}(y)\delta_{x^i}$
identified by Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}, so it needs no separate proof; we state it
because it is the object every experiment in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} measures
against.

\begin{proposition}[Label smoothing never leaves the training set]
\label{prop:atomicity}
For every $h\in[0,\infty)$, $\operatorname{supp}(p_h^{\mathrm{gen}}(\cdot\mid y))
\subseteq\{x^1,\dots,x^N\}$. Consequently, if the true posterior $p(\cdot\mid y)$ is
absolutely continuous,
\begin{equation}
W_2^2\big(p_h^{\mathrm{gen}}(\cdot\mid y),\,p(\cdot\mid y)\big)\ \ge\
\int\operatorname{dist}\big(x,\{x^i\}\big)^2\,p(x\mid y)\,dx\ >\ 0,
\label{eq:w2floor}
\end{equation}
\emph{and this lower bound does not depend on $h$.}
\end{proposition}
This is the central caveat on any ``variance is restored at $h\approx0.1$'' claim:
label smoothing \emph{redistributes weight over training atoms}; it never generates
new samples, so matching $\tr\Covh$ matches a second moment, not the posterior. A
genuine remedy needs $h\to0$ \emph{and} $N\to\infty$ jointly---the Nadaraya--Watson
consistency regime of Proposition~\ref{prop:nwrate} (appendix)---not $h$ alone.

\subsection{Escaping the obstruction: smoothing the endpoint}
\label{sec:theory-remedy}

Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity} rules out any remedy leaving the endpoint law on the
training atoms---label smoothing, and trivially the interpolant noise of
Section~\ref{sec:theory-noise} since $\gamma(1)=0$---and says what a remedy must do:
move the support. Of the three places to inject noise (label, path, endpoint), only
the third does.

\begin{proposition}[Endpoint smoothing]
\label{prop:tgtnoise}
Replace the endpoint by $X_1=x^I+\rho\,\xi$ with $\xi\sim\N(0,I_d)$ independent of
$(I,X_0,\varepsilon)$ and redrawn every step, keeping label bandwidth $h\ge0$. Write
$s_t^2=(1-t)^2+t^2\rho^2$. Then $\vstar$ is again a weighted average of per-atom
fields, now with spatial weights $\varphi_{s_t}(x-tx^i)$ in place of
$\pi_0(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t})$ (explicitly, \eqref{eq:tgtfield} in the appendix), and
the endpoint law is
\begin{equation}
p_1(\cdot\mid y)=\sum_i p_i^{(h)}(y)\,\N(x^i,\rho^2I_d),
\qquad
\bar x=\bar x_h(y),\quad \Cov=\Covh(y)+\rho^2I_d.
\label{eq:tgtendpoint}
\end{equation}
For every $\rho>0$ this law is absolutely continuous, so \eqref{eq:w2floor} does not
apply to it; moreover $s_1=\rho>0$, so $\vstar$ extends continuously to $t=1$ and the
$1/(1-t)$ blow-up of $(\star)$---the source of both Lemma~\ref{lem:norepr} and the
fixed-learning-rate divergence of Section~\ref{sec:exp1}---disappears.
\end{proposition}

Two caveats, both quantified in Section~\ref{sec:p7}: removing a lower bound is not
reducing the distance---\eqref{eq:tgtendpoint} is a kernel density estimate, paying
$+\rho^2I_d$ of variance for its continuity---and the gain is bounded by the
obstruction removed, the floor scaling like $N^{-2/d}$. What is unconditional is the
second knob: $h$ sets $\bar x_h$ and $\Covh$, $\rho$ shifts the covariance by
$\rho^2I_d$, so together they match the posterior's first \emph{and} second moments
with a continuous law, where $h$ alone hits at most one while staying atomic.

The next two results are the one place we leave the fixed-$\D$ setting: they ask what
$\Covh$ and $\bar x_h$ converge to when the training set is itself an i.i.d.\ sample
and $N\to\infty$, which is the only regime in which label smoothing is a genuine
remedy rather than a reweighting.

\begin{proposition}[Bandwidth expansion of the conditional covariance; random design]
\label{prop:expansion}
Let $(x^j,y^j)$ be i.i.d.\ with marginal label density $\rho_Y$, $\Sigma(y)=\Cov(X\mid Y=y)$
and $J(y)=\partial\mu/\partial y$. Fix a query point $y$ in the interior of
$\operatorname{supp}(\rho_Y)$ at which $\rho_Y$ is positive and $C^2$ and $\mu(\cdot)$ is
$C^2$ (standard nonparametric-regression regularity, needed only in a neighbourhood of
this fixed $y$), and assume in addition that $\Sigma(\cdot)$ is \emph{constant} on that
neighbourhood. Then, pointwise at this $y$, as $Nh^k\to\infty,h\to0$,
\begin{equation}
\Covh(y)=\Sigma(y)+h^2 J(y)J(y)^\top+O(h^4)+O_P\big((Nh^k)^{-1/2}\big),
\label{eq:expansion}
\end{equation}
so $\tr\Covh(y)=\tr\Sigma(y)+h^2\|J(y)\|_F^2+\cdots$. Homoscedasticity is
load-bearing, not cosmetic: a varying $\Sigma(\cdot)$ contributes its own $O(h^2)$,
the same order as $h^2JJ^\top$ and not separable from it, so $\|J\|_F^2$ would cease to
be identified (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives the extra term and a counterexample).
The linear-Gaussian instance of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, which is the case we test,
satisfies it exactly with $\Sigma(y)\equiv\Sigmapost$. Nor is the expansion claimed
uniform over $y$; near the boundary of $\operatorname{supp}(\rho_Y)$ the kernel loses mass
asymmetrically and the bias changes order (standard nonparametric boundary bias).
\end{proposition}

The between-group inflation $h^2\|J\|_F^2$ is positive and, unlike the bias of the
conditional \emph{mean}, is not annihilated by kernel symmetry; it produces the
right-hand (over-smoothing) branch of the empirical U-curve. In the linear-Gaussian
model $\mu(y)$ is exactly linear, so $J=\Sigmapost A^\top/\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2$ is
known in closed form and the $O(h^4)$ term vanishes: \eqref{eq:expansion} is then a
parameter-free prediction. Proposition~\ref{prop:nwrate} (appendix) gives the
companion Nadaraya--Watson rates for $\bar x_h(y)$, with $h^\star\asymp
N^{-1/(k+4)}$, and records why queries at $y=y^i$ are \emph{design points}: there
$x^i$ carries the maximal weight $K_h(0)$, so this $h\to0$ limit is a genuinely
different one from Corollary~\ref{cor:zerobw}'s fixed-$N$ collapse---same symbol,
opposite conclusions.

\subsection{Stochastic interpolant noise does not help}
\label{sec:theory-noise}

An equally natural place to inject randomness is the interpolant path itself, as in
stochastic-interpolant constructions. This remedy is qualitatively different: it
never touches the population endpoint, at any noise level. Replace the interpolant by $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1+\gamma(t)Z$ with $Z\sim\N(0,I_d)$
independent and $\gamma(t)=\sigma\sqrt{t(1-t)}$, so $\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=0$. A single
$Z$ defines the whole trajectory, and the correct pathwise-derivative target is
\begin{equation}
\dot X_t=X_1-X_0+\dot\gamma(t)Z,\qquad \dot\gamma(t)=\frac{\sigma(1-2t)}{2\sqrt{t(1-t)}}.
\label{eq:c2target}
\end{equation}
Regressing on $X_1-X_0$ alone is a \emph{different} objective and does not yield the
marginal velocity of the noisy path. Let $s_t^2:=(1-t)^2+\gamma(t)^2=(1-t)[(1-t)+\sigma^2 t]$,
so $s_0=1,\,s_1=0$.

\begin{proposition}[Closed form and endpoint invariance in $\sigma$]
\label{prop:prop17}
Let $h=0$ with distinct labels and $v_\sigma^\star=\E[\dot X_t\mid X_t,t,Y]$ for
target \eqref{eq:c2target}. Then for $t<1$:
(a) $v_\sigma^\star(x,t,y^i)=x^i+c(t)(x-tx^i)$ with
$c(t)=\frac{-(1-t)+\frac{\sigma^2}2(1-2t)}{s_t^2}=\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\log s_t^2$;
(b) the flow is $x_t=t\,x^i+s_t\,x_0$; and
(c) since $s_1=0$,
\begin{equation}
p_1^{\mathrm{cond}}(\cdot\mid y^i)=\delta_{x^i}\quad\text{for every }\sigma\ge0.
\label{eq:invariance}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
Corollary~\ref{cor:prop18} (appendix) records what interpolant noise \emph{does}
change: it raises the irreducible error ($\inf_vL_\sigma>0$ for $\sigma>0$, whereas
$\inf_vL_0=0$) and halves the Lipschitz blow-up rate. Neither touches $p_1$.

\begin{proposition}[The two mechanisms act on different factors]
\label{prop:factors}
In the factorisation \eqref{eq:kernel-field}, label smoothing modifies the label
factor $K_h(y-y^i)$ and therefore changes $p_1$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint});
interpolant noise rescales the spatial factor $\N(tx^i,s_t^2 I_d)$
\emph{isotropically and identically for every $i$}, leaving the endpoint law
unchanged (Proposition~\ref{prop:prop17}). The two remedies are therefore not
interchangeable.
\end{proposition}
This cleanly separates the present conditional mechanism from the unconditional
``resample-$x_0$'' remedy of the gradient-variance line \citep{gradvar2025}, which
operates through attainability rather than through the population optimum.

\subsection{Finite capacity: a genuine representation floor}
\label{sec:theory-floor}

The results so far describe the population minimiser over \emph{all}
square-integrable fields; a trained network is restricted to a finite-capacity
class. Here is one concrete way that restriction alone forces a positive loss floor,
independent of any optimisation difficulty.

\begin{proposition}[Lipschitz lower bound]
\label{prop:liplb}
Fix $i$, $t<1$, $f_i(x,t)=(x^i-x)/(1-t)$, and suppose $v(\cdot,t,y^i)$ is
$L$-Lipschitz in $x$. Under $X_t\mid I=i\sim\N(tx^i,(1-t)^2 I_d)$,
$\E[|v(X_t,t,y^i)-f_i(X_t,t)|^2\mid I=i]\ge d\,[1-L(1-t)]_+^2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Loss floor under a uniform Lipschitz constraint]
\label{cor:floor}
For $\mathcal F_L=\{v:\operatorname{Lip}_x v(\cdot,t,y^i)\le L\ \forall t,i\}$ with
$L\ge1$ and $t\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
\inf_{v\in\mathcal F_L}L_0(v)\ \ge\ d\int_{1-1/L}^1[1-L(1-t)]^2\,dt\ =\ \frac{d}{3L}\ >\ 0.
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Two warnings temper \eqref{eq:floor}. First, the bound is \emph{conditional} on the
constraint: an MLP with unbounded weights lies in no fixed $\mathcal F_L$, and by
universal approximation $\inf_\theta L(v_\theta)$ may be $0$; what we prove is that
any architecture with $\operatorname{Lip}_x v_\theta\le L$ cannot beat $d/(3L)$.
Second, it does not bind in our experiments: with $d=2$ and a trained
$\operatorname{Lip}_x$ of order $10^1$--$10^2$, the floor is of order $10^{-2}$,
one to two orders of magnitude below the loss plateau $0.48$ observed at $2\times10^5$ iterations (it falls to $0.36$ by $7\times10^5$, so the gap is if anything understated).
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor} is therefore best used as a \emph{measurement}---estimate
$L$, compare $d/(3L)$ to the plateau, report which gap dominates
(Appendix~\ref{sec:aux})---and that measurement rules the representation
explanation \emph{out} here.


\subsection{Finite-sample memorisation (summary)}
\label{sec:d2summary}

Appendix~\ref{sec:theory-d2} adapts two representability results of the
unconditional gradient-variance literature \citep{gradvar2025} to the present
notation---a fixed finite \emph{unconditional} batch always admits an exact
zero-loss memorising field despite Proposition~\ref{prop:uncond}'s positive
population loss (Proposition~\ref{prop:memorize}), and resampling $x_0$ every
epoch is exactly what defeats that construction (Remark~\ref{rem:resample})---and
adds a new, problem-specific result: no finite-depth, finite-width network with
globally Lipschitz activations can equal the collapsed field's $1/(1-t)$ blow-up
\emph{pointwise} on all of $[0,1)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:norepr}). We keep that
pointwise claim separate from a loss floor: the only class for which we prove a
strictly positive floor is the Lipschitz-capped $\mathcal F_L$ of
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}. The contrast with Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} is
the point---conditional collapse needs no genericity argument and no fixed-batch
caveat, because $y^i$ pins down $i$ for every $x_0$, not merely with probability
one on a single draw.

\subsection{Scope: what is and is not predicted}
\label{sec:scope}

The population theory proves exact consequences for the empirical minimiser
(Propositions~\ref{prop:collapse}--\ref{prop:expansion},
Corollaries~\ref{cor:zerobw}--\ref{cor:infbw}, Proposition~\ref{prop:liplb},
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}); Appendix~\ref{sec:theory-d2} adds exact statements about
a \emph{randomly drawn} finite unconditional batch. It does \emph{not} determine: the
number of SGD steps to approach the optimum; the dependence of \emph{observed}
collapse on $N$ at fixed architecture (the population optimum collapses for every
finite $N$, so a non-collapsing $N{=}5000$ run probes representation/optimisation, it
does not contradict Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}); the dependence on
$\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}$ (the theory implies no monotonicity, so a flat sweep is
\emph{consistent with}, not a falsification of, the theory); the monotonicity of
$\tr\Covh$ in $h$; or finite-sample error bars. We therefore never call a quantity in
that second list a ``theoretical prediction'' that ``matched'' or ``failed''; it is
reported as an empirical finding, marked ``(empirical).''

\paragraph{Setup.} Unless noted, EXP-1 uses the linear-Gaussian problem
$d{=}2,\,k{=}1,\,N{=}200,\,\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}{=}0.1$, prior and source $\N(0,I)$,

overtraining is the object of study. Sanity checks pass first (the analytic posterior
satisfies the normal equations to $1.8\times10^{-15}$; closed-form field integration
maps every $x_0\to x^i$; the unconditional model does not leak $y$). Sweeps use five
seeds per point. The seed controls \emph{both} the problem instance (the operator $A$,
hence $\Sigmapost$) \emph{and} all training randomness, so every error bar below mixes
the two; Appendix~\ref{sec:seedsplit} separates them with a dedicated
\texttt{problem\_seed} and finds the instance carries $78\%$ of the variance. Every across-seed
$\pm$ we report is a \emph{population} standard deviation (denominator $n$, not
$n{-}1$), applied consistently throughout; at $n{=}5$ the sample standard deviation is
larger by $\sqrt{5/4}\approx1.12$, so a reader preferring that convention should widen
each interval by about $11\%$.

\paragraph{A literature-standard memorisation ratio confirms Corollary~\ref{cor:distinction}.}
The diagnostics above are aggregate (a covariance trace, or distances of the sample
\emph{mean}). As a per-sample complement we report the \emph{memorisation ratio} of
\citet{yoon2023memorization}\footnote{As used, e.g., by \citet{buchanan2025edge}.}:
$\hat x$ is \emph{memorised} if $\|\hat x-x^{(1)}\|^2\le c\|\hat x-x^{(2)}\|^2$ for
$c=1/9$, with $x^{(1)},x^{(2)}$ the two nearest training points. Over 5 seeds both
flows rise from a common $\approx0.11$ baseline, the conditional reaching
$0.650\pm0.033$ at $2\times10^5$ versus $0.389\pm0.032$ unconditionally
(Figure~\ref{fig:memratio})---confirmation that \emph{both} memorise
(Corollary~\ref{cor:distinction}), conditionally more strongly. The two are not
comparable in magnitude---the unconditional target is spread over $N{=}200$ atoms, so
the $c{=}1/9$ test is mechanically harder there---so we read this directionally only.
The scheduled run reaches $0.955\pm0.020$ by $10^6$: 95\% of \emph{individual} samples
are memorised, not merely the sample mean.

\paragraph{Held-out labels: nearest-atom collapse, not generalisation.}
Corollary~\ref{cor:zerobw} predicts what the diagnostics above do not test: at a query
$y$ that is \emph{not} a training label the $h\to0$ endpoint is $\delta_{x^{i^\star}}$
for the nearest label $i^\star$, so held-out conditions should collapse too---onto a
memorised atom, not onto the correct posterior. Over 8 held-out $y$ per run this is
what happens: held-out $\tr\Cov$ tracks the training value (medians $0.43$ vs.\ $0.42$
at $2\times10^5$) while $\|\text{mean}-\mu_{\mathrm{post}}(y)\|$ grows from $0.12$ to
$0.87$ (median): overtraining moves the sampler away from the Bayes answer at
\emph{new} labels too.
We quote medians because in one of five seeds the held-out ODE \emph{diverges},
inflating the mean to $6\times10^4$ (Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}).


\paragraph{Adversarial pairing: collapse does not require a real posterior.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} never uses $A$ or $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}$---only that
the labels are pairwise distinct---so collapse should be unchanged if $Y$ is randomly
permuted relative to $X$ before training. It is: $\tr\Cov$ falls to $0.625\pm0.108$ at
$2\times10^5$ (vs.\ $0.396\pm0.127$ under the real pairing, an optimisation-difficulty
gap at fixed budget), while the distance to $y^i$'s \emph{true} posterior mean
\emph{grows}, $0.84\pm0.13\to1.437\pm0.176$, crossing above $\|\text{mean}-x^i\|$
between $10^4$ and $3\times10^4$ iterations. The generated law tracks the arbitrary
label-to-example assignment, not any statistical relation between $x$ and $y$
(Appendix~\ref{app:adv}).

\subsection{Label smoothing tracks the kernel reference (P7)}
\label{sec:p7}

This is our strongest empirical confirmation of the theory. The correct reference is
$\Covh$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}, Proposition~\ref{prop:moments}), \emph{not}
$\Sigmapost$. Evaluating trained checkpoints against the exact kernel moments over 20
conditions ($M{=}1000$, 5 seeds), the generated variance tracks $\Covh$ at every
bandwidth: ratio-to-kernel $=1.002\pm0.050$, $0.994\pm0.039$ and $1.011\pm0.022$ at
$h=0.05,0.1,0.5$ (Figure~\ref{fig:p7summary}; Table~\ref{tab:p7kernel}, appendix,
gives all entries). This is a verification of the \emph{endpoint law}, stronger than
a velocity-field check. At $h{=}0.01$ the ratio is high and unstable
($1.308\pm0.376$) because $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx3.5$ leaves $\Covh$ near-degenerate
for many conditions; the ratio of mean traces, $0.673/0.583=1.15$, is closer to $1$.



A natural but mistaken reading is that $h\approx0.1$ ``perfectly restores'' the
posterior variance: $\tr\Cov$ merely crosses $\Sigmapost\approx1.02$ near $h{=}0.1$
because for this problem $\Sigmapost\approx\Covh|_{h=0.1}\approx1.00$---a coincidence.
The correct target is $\Covh$, which the model attains at \emph{every} $h$. The field
itself agrees: $v_\theta$ matches the kernel minimiser \eqref{eq:kernel-field}
$2$--$4\times$ better than the single-atom field $(\star)$ at every $h$, and the gap
widens with $h$ (Table~\ref{tab:dagger} and Figure~\ref{fig:kernel}, appendix).

\paragraph{Atomicity caveat (empirical confirmation of
Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity}).} Even at the best-matching $h$ the generated law
remains atomic: $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx26/200$ at $h{=}0.1$ and $\approx102/200$ at
$h{=}0.5$, and the MMD to the analytic posterior falls with $h$ ($0.256\to0.012$) but
reaches zero at no $h$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:aux}). Matching variance is not recovering
the posterior.

\paragraph{How large is the atomicity floor, and does endpoint smoothing help?}
Unlike the MMD, the floor \eqref{eq:w2floor} is directly computable: at the EXP-1
configuration $F=0.0353$, i.e.\ $W_2\ge0.19$ but only $3.5\%$ of $\tr\Sigmapost$, and
the \emph{population-optimal} atomic law already reaches ${\rm MMD}=0.0089$---below the
trained model's $0.024$. Atomicity, though exact, is therefore \emph{not} what the MMD
curves measure; we correct that misreading in Appendix~\ref{sec:aux}. The reason is
instrumental: an RBF MMD at the median-heuristic bandwidth cannot resolve inter-atom
spacing. Scored in an entropic OT divergence, which can, endpoint smoothing
(Proposition~\ref{prop:tgtnoise}) cuts the population distance to the posterior
$2.1\times$ at $h{=}0.1$ ($12.0\to5.7$ at $\rho{=}0.1$), with the expected U-shape in
$\rho$, while MMD moves by at most $1.2\times$. \emph{A trained model attains this}:
retraining EXP-1 at $N{=}50$ (where the $d{=}2$ floor is largest,
$F/\tr\Sigmapost=0.19$) with $h{=}0.1$ and $\rho\in\{0,0.3\}$, the generated $\tr\Cov$
matches \eqref{eq:tgtendpoint}'s $\tr\Covh+d\rho^2$ to within $3\%$ in both arms and
the mean is unchanged, while the OT distance falls $2.5\times$ ($79.2\to31.5$, and
$2.5\times$ in each seed) against only $1.5\times$ in MMD
(Appendix~\ref{app:tgt}). Table~\ref{tab:floor} sweeps $(d,N)$ and adds the caveat
that matters: the gain grows with the floor at fixed $d$ but \emph{not} across
$d$---at $d{=}10$ the floor is largest and the gain among the smallest, since an
isotropic $\rho$ cannot fill a high-dimensional gap without paying $d\rho^2$. Endpoint
smoothing is defeated by dimension, as kernel density estimation is.

\paragraph{Interpolant noise does not help (Proposition~\ref{prop:prop17}).} With the
\emph{corrected} target \eqref{eq:c2target} (verified by four closed-form checks) the
generated variance is unchanged from that obtained with the mis-specified $X_1-X_0$
target ($0.367\pm0.146$ vs.\ $0.372\pm0.140$ at $\sigma{=}0.1$; Table~\ref{tab:p7i},
appendix) and stays far below the posterior---exactly as \eqref{eq:invariance}
predicts, the endpoint law being $\delta_{x^i}$ for every $\sigma$ whether or not the
target is correct.

\subsection{Realistic capacity: CIFAR-10 with a 35.75M-parameter DDPM U-Net}
\label{sec:cifarddpm}

Everything above is $d\le10$ synthetic or a $0.5$M U-Net, so the standing objection is
that the mechanism is an artefact of small capacity. We therefore repeat EXP-3 with the
standard DDPM U-Net (base width $128$, channel multipliers $(1,2,2,2)$, self-attention
at $16{\times}16$; $35.75$M parameters), on CIFAR-10 bottom-half inpainting with
$N{=}2000$, $d{=}3072$ and $k{=}1536$, for $60000$ iterations at batch $128$. That is
$3840$ gradient samples per training image, the same per-image exposure as the
$N$-sweep of Appendix~\ref{sec:nsweep3}, so collapse depth is comparable to it.
Evaluation is at $48$ conditions with $M{=}256$ samples each, drawn from the final
checkpoint, with the unbiased ($n{-}1$) covariance. Sample size matters here: the trace
of a covariance estimated from $M$ samples carries a relative sampling error of about
$\sqrt{2/(M-1)}$, which is $8.9\%$ at $M{=}256$ but $36\%$ at the $M{=}16$ used during
training, so all numbers below are re-measured from checkpoints rather than read off the
training-time metrics.

\paragraph{Hard conditioning ($h{=}0$): Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} at scale.}
$\tr\Cov$ falls from $179.1$ at iteration $500$ to $0.431$ at $60000$ on the
training-time estimator, a factor of $415$; re-measured at $M{=}256$ the final value is
$0.458$. Every one of the $48$ conditions has $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=1.00$, the generated
mean identifies the correct training image at $48/48$ conditions, and the memorisation
ratio is exactly $1.000$. At $35.75$M parameters on real images, the trained model does
what Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} says the population minimiser does.

\paragraph{Label smoothing ($h>0$): the aggregate ratio is the wrong statistic.}
Table~\ref{tab:cifarddpm} reports, per bandwidth, both the ratio
$\tr\Cov/\tr\Covh$ the earlier sections use and the regression that we argue should
replace it: the OLS slope $\beta$ of $\log\tr\Cov$ on $\log\tr\Covh$ \emph{across
conditions}. Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint} predicts $\beta=1$; $\beta=0$ would mean the
model ignores the reference entirely.

a factor that itself grows with the reference.

\paragraph{The atomicity floor does not survive training.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity} proves that the smoothed population law is supported on
the training set for \emph{every} $h$. The trained network escapes it, progressively: the
fraction of generated samples lying on a training atom is $1.000$, $0.829$, $0.717$ and
$0.620$ at $h=0,4,5,6$, so at $h{=}6$ roughly $38\%$ of samples are off-atom. The value
of exactly $1.000$ at $h{=}0$ rules out ODE discretisation as the explanation, since the
same integrator is used throughout. Atomicity is thus a property of the population
minimiser that finite capacity partially undoes---which is good news for sample quality
and bad news for treating Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity} as a statement about trained
models. It is also the one place in this paper where a proved property of the exact
minimiser is measurably violated by the network that approximates it.

\paragraph{What governs the discrepancy is $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$, not $h$.}
Pooling all $144$ (condition, $h$) pairs with $h>0$, the Spearman correlation between
the ratio and the effective sample size $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/\sum_jp_j^2$ is $-0.858$,
against $-0.554$ for the correlation with $h$ itself. The bandwidth matters only through
how many training atoms it actually pools; conditions whose kernel weight concentrates on
one or two atoms are the ones the model overshoots, at any $h$.

\paragraph{Reproducibility across problem instances.} Repeating the $h{=}0$ and $h{=}4$
runs at a second seed---which redraws the mask, the training subset and all training
randomness---moves $\tr\Cov$ at $h{=}4$ from $178.5$ to $240.7$ ($35\%$) and
$n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ from $2.36$ to $2.77$, so the two instances are genuinely different
problems; the ratio to the Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint} reference moves from $6.83$ to
$7.07$, i.e.\ by $3.5\%$ (training-time estimator, used consistently for both). At
$h{=}0$, $\tr\Cov$ is $0.431$ against $0.409$. The reference-relative quantity is the
stable one, which is the practical case for reporting it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{A proved property of the population minimiser that the trained model
violates.} Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity} shows the smoothed law is supported on the
training set for every $h$, and Section~\ref{sec:cifarddpm} measures the fraction of
generated samples actually on an atom as $1.000$, $0.829$, $0.717$, $0.620$ at
$h=0,4,5,6$. The obstruction is therefore exact for the object the theory describes and
only approximate for the object practitioners train, with the gap widening as $h$ grows.
We do not have a quantitative account of that gap, and it is the clearest instance in
this paper of the population/finite-network distinction biting: everywhere else the two
agree in direction, here they disagree about a set-valued statement that is either true
or false.

\paragraph{The optimisation gap is bounded away from one explanation, not explained.}
A finite network reaches the population minimiser only up to an optimisation gap,
which is why hard-conditioning collapse is \emph{optimisation-paced} and only partial
within a finite budget (for $h>0$ the model does reach $\Covh$).
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}'s Lipschitz floor was our candidate explanation for the
$h{=}0$ plateau, and Appendix~\ref{sec:aux} shows it does \emph{not} bind: it is
$\sim\!50\times$ below the plateau, which is moreover strongly schedule-sensitive
(Table~\ref{tab:exp1}). We have thus ruled \emph{this} mechanism out without
supplying a positive quantitative theory of the gap itself.

\paragraph{Scale, and a remedy of limited reach.} EXP-1/EXP-2 are $d\le10$ synthetic
problems and EXP-3's MNIST runs use $32\times32$ images, $N{=}500$ and one small U-Net.
Section~\ref{sec:cifarddpm} extends this to the standard DDPM U-Net at $35.75$M
parameters on CIFAR-10 ($d{=}3072$), where hard-conditioning collapse is unambiguous
($415\times$ in $\tr\Cov$, correct training image at $48/48$ conditions); but the
$h>0$ agreement there is only partial, with an across-condition log--log slope of
$0.481$ against the predicted $1$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint} is confirmed in
direction and not in magnitude at that scale. Beyond $35.75$M parameters, and for
text-conditional models where the label space is not a Euclidean space with a natural
kernel, persistence remains a claim we do not establish. The width/depth/optimiser
sweep (Appendix~\ref{app:exp}) covers only $\tr\Cov$ at one budget. Endpoint smoothing (Proposition~\ref{prop:tgtnoise}) removes the atomicity
obstruction by construction and is confirmed on a trained model at $N{=}50$, but its
benefit is bounded by that obstruction's size---small in exactly the regime we study
most---it buys continuity at the price of $+\rho^2I_d$ of variance, and it degrades
with dimension (Table~\ref{tab:floor}); we have not tested it at scale, on EXP-2/EXP-3,
or with an anisotropic $\rho$. Finally, P5/P6 are empirical
findings the theory does not determine, and the checkpoint-based tables require
retraining the $p7y$ runs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Conditional collapse in flow matching is the zero-bandwidth limit of a kernel-weighted
empirical conditional flow, and label smoothing makes that flow exact kernel regression
over the training atoms. Neither label nor interpolant noise leaves the atomic support;
endpoint smoothing does, but only as far as the floor it removes is large.

\begin{lemma}[Change of variables]
\label{lem:cov}
Fix $i$ and $t<1$. Conditionally on $I=i$, the map $X_0\mapsto X_t=(1-t)X_0+tx^i$ is an
invertible affine bijection of $\R^d$ with inverse $X_0=(X_t-tx^i)/(1-t)$; hence
$\sigma(X_t\mid I=i)=\sigma(X_0\mid I=i)$ and
\begin{equation}
p(x\mid I=i,t)=(1-t)^{-d}\,\pi_0\!\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big).
\label{eq:cov}
\end{equation}
The Jacobian factor $(1-t)^{-d}$ does not depend on $i$, so it cancels in every
posterior over $I$. Because $\pi_0$ is Gaussian, $p(x\mid I=i,t)>0$ for every $x$ and
every $t<1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The map $\Psi(x_0)=(1-t)x_0+tx^i$ has linear part $(1-t)I_d$, invertible for $t<1$, with
$\det D\Psi=(1-t)^d$ and inverse $\Psi^{-1}(x)=(x-tx^i)/(1-t)$; a bijection generates the
same $\sigma$-algebra as its argument, and \eqref{eq:cov} is the change-of-variables
formula for densities, $p_{X_t}(x)=p_{X_0}(\Psi^{-1}(x))\,|\det D\Psi|^{-1}$. The factor
$(1-t)^{-d}$ involves neither $i$ nor $x$, so it appears identically in numerator and
denominator of $\Pr(I=i\mid X_t=x,t)$. Positivity is that of the Gaussian density
$\pi_0>0$ everywhere, composed with a bijection.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Well-posedness]
\label{lem:wellposed}
Let $w_1,\dots,w_N:\R^d\times[0,1)\to[0,1]$ be $C^\infty$ with $\sum_i w_i\equiv1$, and
set $v(x,t)=\sum_i w_i(x,t)(x^i-x)/(1-t)$ and $M=\max_i|x^i|$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item $v\in C^\infty(\R^d\times[0,1))$, hence is locally Lipschitz in $x$, uniformly on
  compact subsets of $[0,1)$;
\item for every $x_0$ the ODE $\dot x_t=v(x_t,t)$, $x_{t=0}=x_0$, has a unique solution
  on the whole of $[0,1)$, and it obeys $|x_t|+M\le(|x_0|+M)/(1-t)$;
\item writing $\Phi_{t}$ for the resulting flow map, for every $\mu_0\in\mathcal P(\R^d)$
  the curve $\mu_t:=(\Phi_t)_\#\mu_0$ is the \emph{unique} narrowly continuous measure
  solution of $\partial_t\mu_t+\nabla\!\cdot(v\mu_t)=0$ on $[0,1)$ with initial datum
  $\mu_0$.
\end{enumerate}
We do \emph{not} claim that $\lim_{t\uparrow1}\mu_t$ exists for arbitrary such weights;
where an endpoint law is asserted below (Propositions~\ref{prop:collapse}
and~\ref{prop:kernel-field}, Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}) its existence is established
directly, from almost-sure convergence of the interpolant, and $p_1$ denotes that weak
limit.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) On $\R^d\times[0,1)$ the map $(x,t)\mapsto(x^i-x)/(1-t)$ is $C^\infty$ and the $w_i$
are $C^\infty$ by hypothesis, so $v$ is a finite sum of products of $C^\infty$ functions.
Local Lipschitzness in $x$, uniform for $t$ in compacts of $[0,1)$, follows from
continuity of $\nabla_x v$ on the compact set.

(b) Local existence and uniqueness on a maximal interval $[0,\tau)$ is
Picard--Lindel\"of \citep[Ch.~II]{hartman2002ode}, using (a). For the bound, note
$\sum_i w_i\equiv1$ and $w_i\ge0$ give the pointwise estimate
\begin{equation}
|v(x,t)|\;\le\;\sum_i w_i(x,t)\,\frac{|x^i-x|}{1-t}\;\le\;\frac{M+|x|}{1-t}.
\label{eq:vgrowth}
\end{equation}
Put $g(t):=|x_t|+M$, which is locally Lipschitz, hence differentiable a.e.\ with
$g'(t)\le|\dot x_t|=|v(x_t,t)|\le g(t)/(1-t)$ by \eqref{eq:vgrowth}. Gr\"onwall's
inequality gives
\[
g(t)\;\le\;g(0)\exp\!\int_0^t\frac{ds}{1-s}\;=\;\frac{g(0)}{1-t},
\qquad t\in[0,\tau),
\]
which is the stated bound. Since the right-hand side is finite for every $t<1$, the
solution stays in a bounded set on every $[0,T]$ with $T<1$, so it cannot blow up before
$t=1$ and $\tau=1$.

(c) That $\mu_t=(\Phi_t)_\#\mu_0$ solves the continuity equation is the standard
computation: for $\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\R^d)$,
$\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi\,d\mu_t=\int\nabla\varphi(\Phi_t(x))\cdot v(\Phi_t(x),t)\,
d\mu_0(x)=\int\nabla\varphi\cdot v\,d\mu_t$. For uniqueness, fix $T<1$ and let
$(\mu_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$ be any narrowly continuous solution with the same initial datum.
By (a) and (b) the flow is a $C^\infty$ diffeomorphism of $\R^d$ for each $t\le T$
\citep[Ch.~V]{hartman2002ode}; write $\Phi_{s,T}$ for the flow from time $s$ to time
$T$. Given $\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\R^d)$, set $\psi(x,s):=\varphi(\Phi_{s,T}(x))$. Then
$\psi$ is $C^1$ on $\R^d\times[0,T]$, solves the backward transport equation
$\partial_s\psi+v\cdot\nabla\psi=0$ with $\psi(\cdot,T)=\varphi$ by the method of
characteristics, and has compact support in $x$ for each $s$, namely
$\Phi_{T,s}(\operatorname{supp}\varphi)$. Testing the continuity equation against $\psi$
(legitimate after the usual truncation in time, since $\psi$ is $C^1$ and compactly
supported in $x$ uniformly on $[0,T]$),
\[
\frac{d}{ds}\int\psi(\cdot,s)\,d\mu_s
=\int\big(\partial_s\psi+v\cdot\nabla\psi\big)\,d\mu_s=0 ,
\]
so $\int\varphi\,d\mu_T=\int\psi(\cdot,0)\,d\mu_0=\int\varphi\circ\Phi_{0,T}\,d\mu_0
=\int\varphi\,d\big((\Phi_T)_\#\mu_0\big)$. As $\varphi$ was arbitrary,
$\mu_T=(\Phi_T)_\#\mu_0$, and $T<1$ was arbitrary. (This is the classical
smooth-field case of the general uniqueness theory for continuity equations; see
\citealp[Ch.~8]{ambrosio2008gradient} for the statement under far weaker regularity.)
\end{proof}

Both $v_{\mathrm{unc}}^\star$ and $\vstar$ have this form with smooth weights, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:wellposed} applies and no ``assume the flow is well defined'' hypothesis
is needed. We now give the remaining proofs in full.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:uncond}]
Equation~\eqref{eq:distinction}'s $q_i(x,t)$ is Bayes' rule applied to
Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}'s density $p(x\mid I=i,t)=(1-t)^{-d}\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)$
under the uniform prior on $I$: the Jacobian $(1-t)^{-d}$ and the prior $1/N$ do not
depend on $i$ and cancel, leaving $q_i(x,t)\propto\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)$.
Given $I=i$, Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}(a)'s argument gives
$U=(x^i-x)/(1-t)$; averaging over the posterior $q_i(x,t)$ and substituting into
Proposition~\ref{prop:projection} gives $v_{\mathrm{unc}}^\star$. For the endpoint
law: by construction $v_{\mathrm{unc}}^\star(x,t)=\E[\dot X_t\mid X_t=x,t]$, which is
exactly the velocity field appearing in the continuity equation for the marginal law
of the interpolation. By Lemma~\ref{lem:wellposed} the flow is well posed on $[0,1)$
and transports $p_0=\pi_0$ to $p_t=\mathrm{Law}(X_t)$ for every $t<1$; since
$X_t\to X_1\sim\rhohat_X$ almost surely, $p_t\Rightarrow\rhohat_X$, giving
$p_1^{\mathrm{unc}}=\rhohat_X$.

For $\inf_v L_{\mathrm{unc}}>0$: fix $t<1$ and $x$. By the remark after
Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}, $\pi_0$ is everywhere positive, so $q_i(x,t)>0$ for every $i$.
Conditionally on $(X_t=x,t,I=i)$ the target is the deterministic vector
$u_i=(x^i-x)/(1-t)$, and $u_i\ne u_j$ whenever $x^i\ne x^j$. Hence the discrete law
$\{q_i(x,t)\}_i$ places positive mass on at least two distinct values $u_i\ne u_j$
whenever some $x^i\ne x^j$, so
$\Var(U\mid X_t=x,t)=\sum_i q_i(x,t)\,|u_i-\bar u(x,t)|^2>0$
at \emph{every} $(x,t)$ with $t<1$, where $\bar u(x,t)=\sum_i q_i(x,t)u_i$.
Integrating over $(x,t)$ and applying Proposition~\ref{prop:projection},
$\inf_v L_{\mathrm{unc}}(v)=\E[\Var(U\mid X_t,t)]>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity}]
The support claim is Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}. For any coupling $(X,X')$ of
$p_h^{\mathrm{gen}}$ and $p(\cdot\mid y)$, $X\in\{x^i\}$ a.s.\ so
$|X-X'|\ge\operatorname{dist}(X',\{x^i\})$ pointwise; take expectations and infimise.
The right side is the same for every $h$ and is strictly positive because a finite
set is $p(\cdot\mid y)$-null.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:infbw}]
$K_h(y-y^i)=(2\pi h^2)^{-k/2}\big[1+O(h^{-2})\big]$ uniformly in $i$ as $h\to\infty$,
so the normalised weights $p_i^{(h)}(y)\to1/N$; apply Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:zerobw}]
For $i\ne i^\star$,
$K_h(y-y^i)/K_h(y-y^{i^\star})=\exp\big[-(|y-y^i|^2-|y-y^{i^\star}|^2)/2h^2\big]\to0$
as $h\to0$, since $|y-y^i|>|y-y^{i^\star}|$; normalising the weights $K_h(y-y^j)$
gives $p^{(h)}(y)\to\mathbf 1\{i=i^\star\}$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint} gives the
claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:mixture-coupling}, $\vstar(\cdot,\cdot,y)$ is exactly the
conditional velocity field of the mixture coupling $I\sim p^{(h)}(y)$,
$X_0\sim\pi_0$, $X_1=x^I$, driven by the same linear interpolant. This field has
the form $v(x,t)=\sum_iw_i(x,t)(x^i-x)/(1-t)$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:wellposed}, with
smooth weights $w_i=w_i^{(h)}(x,t,y)$ summing to one (a ratio of smooth, strictly
positive functions of $(x,t)$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}'s positivity of $\pi_0$), so
Lemma~\ref{lem:wellposed} applies: the ODE $\dot x_t=\vstar(x_t,t,y)$ is well posed
on $[0,1)$ for every initial condition, and by the superposition principle its flow
$(\Phi_t)_{t<1}$ transports $\pi_0=\mathrm{Law}(X_0)$ to
$p_t:=\mathrm{Law}(X_t)$ for every $t<1$, where $X_t$ is the coupling's own
interpolant---because $\vstar$ is by construction the coupling's conditional
velocity, $(\Phi_t)_\#\pi_0$ and $\mathrm{Law}(X_t)$ solve the same continuity
equation with the same initial condition, hence coincide by uniqueness. It remains
to identify the limiting law of $X_t$ as $t\uparrow1$: since $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tx^I$
with $X_0\sim\pi_0$ independent of $I$, $X_t\to x^I$ almost surely as $t\uparrow1$
for every realisation of $I$; hence $\mathrm{Law}(X_t)\Rightarrow\mathrm{Law}(x^I)
=\sum_ip_i^{(h)}(y)\delta_{x^i}$ weakly, which is $p_1=\lim_{t\uparrow1}p_t$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:wellposed}. This is \eqref{eq:endpoint}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Equivalent mixture coupling]
\label{lem:mixture-coupling}
Fix $y$ and consider the coupling $I\sim p^{(h)}(y)$, $X_0\sim\pi_0$ (independent of
$I$), $X_1:=x^I$, with the same linear interpolant $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$. Its
conditional velocity field $\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x,t]$ equals $\vstar(x,t,y)$ of
\eqref{eq:kernel-field} for every $x,t<1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:mixture-coupling}]
Under this coupling, $\Pr(I=i\mid X_t=x,t)\propto\Pr(I=i)\,p(x\mid I=i,t)
=p_i^{(h)}(y)\cdot(1-t)^{-d}\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)$ by the same Bayes'-rule
computation as above (now with prior $p_i^{(h)}(y)$ in place of $1/N$), and
$p_i^{(h)}(y)\propto K_h(y-y^i)$ by definition, so
$\Pr(I=i\mid X_t=x,t)\propto\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)K_h(y-y^i)$---the same
proportionality (in $i$, at fixed $x,t,y$) as the numerator of $w_i^{(h)}$ in
\eqref{eq:kernel-field}; normalising over $i$ recovers $w_i^{(h)}(x,t,y)$ exactly.
As in the proof above, given $I=i$ the target is $(x^i-x)/(1-t)$, so
$\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x,t]=\sum_iw_i^{(h)}(x,t,y)(x^i-x)/(1-t)=\vstar(x,t,y)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:kernel-field}]
Fix $(x,t,y)$ with $t<1$. By Bayes' rule applied to the discrete latent index $I$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr(I=i\mid X_t=x,t,\widetilde Y=y)=
\frac{\Pr(I=i)\,p(x,y\mid I=i,t)}{\sum_j\Pr(I=j)\,p(x,y\mid I=j,t)}.
\label{eq:bayes-index}
\end{equation}
Given $I=i$, $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tx^i$ is a deterministic affine function of $X_0$ alone
and $\widetilde Y=y^i+h\varepsilon$ is a deterministic affine function of $\varepsilon$
alone, with $X_0\perp\varepsilon$; hence $X_t\perp\widetilde Y\mid I=i$, with
marginal conditional densities $p(x\mid I=i,t)=(1-t)^{-d}\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}) and $p(y\mid I=i)=K_h(y-y^i)$ (the density of $y^i+h\varepsilon$).
Independence gives the joint conditional density
$p(x,y\mid I=i,t)=(1-t)^{-d}\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)K_h(y-y^i)$; with the
uniform prior $\Pr(I=i)=1/N$, \eqref{eq:bayes-index} becomes
\begin{align*}
\Pr(I=i\mid X_t=x,t,\widetilde Y=y)
&=\frac{\frac1N(1-t)^{-d}\,\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)K_h(y-y^i)}
{\sum_j\frac1N(1-t)^{-d}\,\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^j}{1-t}\big)K_h(y-y^j)}\\
&=\frac{\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^i}{1-t}\big)K_h(y-y^i)}
{\sum_j\pi_0\big(\tfrac{x-tx^j}{1-t}\big)K_h(y-y^j)}=w_i^{(h)}(x,t,y),
\end{align*}
the $i$-independent factor $\frac1N(1-t)^{-d}$ cancelling between the numerator and
every term of the denominator (the kernel normalisation constant inside $K_h$
cancels identically for the same reason). Given $I=i$, one further conditions on
$X_t=x$: by Lemma~\ref{lem:cov} this determines $X_0=(x-tx^i)/(1-t)$, so
$U=X_1-X_0=x^i-X_0=(x^i-x)/(1-t)$, exactly as in
Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}(a). By Proposition~\ref{prop:projection}, the
population minimiser is $\vstar(x,t,y)=\E[U\mid X_t=x,t,\widetilde Y=y]$, obtained
by averaging this per-index target over the index posterior just computed:
\[
\vstar(x,t,y)=\sum_{i=1}^N\Pr(I=i\mid X_t=x,t,\widetilde Y=y)\cdot\frac{x^i-x}{1-t}
=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_i^{(h)}(x,t,y)\,\frac{x^i-x}{1-t},
\]
which is \eqref{eq:kernel-field}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:expansion}]
Throughout, $\rho_Y$ denotes the marginal density of $Y$ and $s(y):=\nabla\log\rho_Y(y)\in\R^k$
its score at $y$, both well defined since $\rho_Y$ is positive and $C^2$ near $y$.

\emph{Step 0: an exact identity via a tilted population measure.} Define the
$h$-tilted density of $Y$,
\[
q_h(y'):=\frac{K_h(y-y')\,\rho_Y(y')}{\int K_h(y-y'')\,\rho_Y(y'')\,dy''},
\]
and, for a measurable $f$, $\E_h[f(X,Y)]:=\E[K_h(y-Y)f(X,Y)]/\E[K_h(y-Y)]$, i.e.\
expectation under $Y\sim q_h$ with $X\mid Y$ unchanged. As $N\to\infty$ the empirical
kernel-weighted moments $\bar x_h(y),\Covh(y)$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:moments}
converge to their population counterparts $\E_h[X]$, $\Cov_h^{\mathrm{pop}}(y):=\Cov_h[X]$
(a law of large numbers for the weighted sums $\sum_jK_h(y-y^j)x^j$ and
$\sum_jK_h(y-y^j)$ separately, then Slutsky's theorem for the ratio); the discrepancy
between the finite-$N$ empirical moments and this population limit is the
$O_P((Nh^k)^{-1/2})$ term addressed in Step~3. It therefore suffices to show
\begin{equation}
\Cov_h^{\mathrm{pop}}(y)=\Sigma(y)+h^2J(y)J(y)^\top+O(h^4).
\label{eq:expansion-pop}
\end{equation}
By the law of total covariance applied to $X$ given $Y$ under the tilted law $Y\sim q_h$,
\begin{equation}
\Cov_h^{\mathrm{pop}}(y)=\underbrace{\E_h[\Sigma(Y)]}_{\text{within-group}}
+\underbrace{\Cov_h[\mu(Y)]}_{\text{between-group}},
\label{eq:expansion-totcov}
\end{equation}
which is an exact identity for every $h>0$, since $\Sigma(Y)=\Cov(X\mid Y)$ and
$\mu(Y)=\E[X\mid Y]$ by definition and the law of total covariance requires no
approximation.

\emph{Step 1: the tilted law of $Y$, rescaled.} Substitute $y'=y+hu$
($dy'=h^k\,du$) and write $K_h(y-y')=h^{-k}\varphi(u)$ with
$\varphi(u)=(2\pi)^{-k/2}e^{-|u|^2/2}$ the standard Gaussian density (immediate from
the definition of $K_h$). Then
\[
\int K_h(y-y'')\rho_Y(y'')\,dy''=\int\varphi(u)\rho_Y(y+hu)\,du,
\]
and by the $C^2$ Taylor expansion $\rho_Y(y+hu)=\rho_Y(y)\big[1+h\,s(y)^\top u+O(h^2)\big]$
(uniformly over the region where $\varphi$ has non-negligible mass, using $C^2$
regularity of $\rho_Y$ near $y$), together with $\int\varphi(u)u\,du=0$,
\[
\int\varphi(u)\rho_Y(y+hu)\,du=\rho_Y(y)\big[1+O(h^2)\big].
\]
Hence the tilted law of $u:=(Y-y)/h$ under $q_h$ has density
\begin{equation}
\tilde q_h(u)=\frac{\varphi(u)\rho_Y(y+hu)}{\int\varphi(u')\rho_Y(y+hu')\,du'}
=\varphi(u)\big[1+h\,s(y)^\top u+O(h^2)\big].
\label{eq:tilted-u}
\end{equation}
Using $\E_\varphi[u]=0$, $\E_\varphi[uu^\top]=I_k$, $\E_\varphi[u_au_bu_c]=0$ (odd
moments of a centred Gaussian vanish) and $\E_\varphi[u_au_bu_cu_d]=\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}
+\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}+\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}$, \eqref{eq:tilted-u} gives the moments
\begin{align}
\E_{\tilde q_h}[u]&=\E_\varphi[u]+h\,\E_\varphi\!\big[u\,(s^\top u)\big]+O(h^2)
=h\,\E_\varphi[uu^\top]s+O(h^2)=h\,s(y)+O(h^2),
\label{eq:u-mean}\\
\E_{\tilde q_h}[uu^\top]&=\E_\varphi[uu^\top]+h\,\E_\varphi\!\big[uu^\top(s^\top u)\big]+O(h^2)
=I_k+0+O(h^2),
\label{eq:u-2mom}
\end{align}
the vanishing middle term in \eqref{eq:u-2mom} because $\E_\varphi[u_au_bu_c]=0$ for
every $a,b,c$. Consequently
\begin{equation}
\Cov_{\tilde q_h}[u]=\E_{\tilde q_h}[uu^\top]-\E_{\tilde q_h}[u]\E_{\tilde q_h}[u]^\top
=I_k+O(h^2)-h^2s(y)s(y)^\top=I_k+O(h^2).
\label{eq:u-cov}
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 2: within-group term.} Here the homoscedasticity hypothesis is used, and
it is used for the whole of this step: with $\Sigma(\cdot)\equiv\Sigma(y)$ on a
neighbourhood of $y$, and since $\tilde q_h$ places all but exponentially little mass
on that neighbourhood,
\[
\E_h[\Sigma(Y)]=\E_{\tilde q_h}[\Sigma(y+hu)]=\Sigma(y),
\]
exactly, with no remainder. This is the within-group term of \eqref{eq:expansion}.

It is worth recording what fails without that hypothesis, since the gap is of leading
order rather than a technicality. For merely $C^2$ $\Sigma(\cdot)$ one gets, by the same
substitution together with \eqref{eq:u-mean} and \eqref{eq:u-2mom},
\[
\E_h[\Sigma(Y)]=\Sigma(y)+h\,\big[\nabla\Sigma(y)\cdot\E_{\tilde q_h}[u]\big]
+\tfrac{h^2}2\big[\nabla^2\Sigma(y)\cdot\E_{\tilde q_h}[uu^\top]\big]+O(h^3)
=\Sigma(y)+h^2\Big[\nabla\Sigma(y)\cdot s(y)+\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\nabla^2\Sigma(y)\Big]+O(h^3),
\]
using $\E_{\tilde q_h}[u]=h\,s(y)+O(h^2)$. The bracket is an $O(1)$ quantity multiplying
$h^2$, i.e.\ exactly the order of the between-group term $h^2JJ^\top$ computed in
Step~3, so the two cannot be separated and $\|J\|_F^2$ is not identifiable from
$\tr\Covh$ alone. A one-dimensional example makes the failure concrete: take
$k=d=1$, $Y\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-2,2)$ and $X\mid Y=y\sim\N(0,1+y^2)$, so that
$\mu\equiv0$ and $J\equiv0$. Then $\Covh(y)=\E_{\tilde q_h}[1+(y+hu)^2]=\Sigma(y)+h^2$,
whereas \eqref{eq:expansion} with $J=0$ would predict $\Sigma(y)+O(h^4)$.

\emph{Step 3: between-group term.} By the $C^2$ Taylor expansion of $\mu$,
\[
\mu(y+hu)=\mu(y)+hJ(y)u+\tfrac{h^2}{2}Q(u)+O(h^3),\qquad
Q(u)_a:=\sum_{b,c}\partial^2_{y_by_c}\mu_a(y)\,u_bu_c,
\]
and since covariance is invariant to the additive constant $\mu(y)$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Cov_h[\mu(Y)]&=\Cov_{\tilde q_h}\!\Big[hJ(y)u+\tfrac{h^2}2Q(u)+O(h^3)\Big]\\
&=h^2J(y)\Cov_{\tilde q_h}[u]J(y)^\top
+h^3\Big[J(y)\Cov_{\tilde q_h}(u,Q(u))+\mathrm{sym}\Big]
+h^4\Cov_{\tilde q_h}[Q(u)].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:expansion-terms}
\end{equation}
The leading term is $h^2J(y)\big[I_k+O(h^2)\big]J(y)^\top=h^2J(y)J(y)^\top+O(h^4)$ by
\eqref{eq:u-cov}. It remains to show the middle, apparently $O(h^3)$, cross term is
in fact $O(h^4)$: this holds because $\Cov_{\tilde q_h}(u,Q(u))$ itself vanishes at
$h=0$ and is only $O(h)$, not $O(1)$. Indeed, since $Q(u)$ is a quadratic form in
$u$, $\E_{\tilde q_h}[u\,Q(u)^\top]$ and $\E_{\tilde q_h}[u]\,\E_{\tilde q_h}[Q(u)]^\top$
both involve only the third moments $\E_{\tilde q_h}[u_au_bu_c]$ of $\tilde q_h$ (for
the second factor, through $\E_{\tilde q_h}[u]=O(h)$ paired with
$\E_{\tilde q_h}[Q(u)]=O(1)$, which is itself an $O(h)$ quantity overall); expanding
\eqref{eq:tilted-u} to the relevant order,
\begin{align*}
\E_{\tilde q_h}[u_au_bu_c]
&=\E_\varphi[u_au_bu_c]+h\sum_e s_e(y)\,\E_\varphi[u_au_bu_cu_e]+O(h^2)\\
&=0+h\big[s_c(y)\delta_{ab}+s_b(y)\delta_{ac}+s_a(y)\delta_{bc}\big]+O(h^2),
\end{align*}
using $\E_\varphi[u_au_bu_c]=0$ and the fourth-moment identity above. Thus every
third moment of $\tilde q_h$ is itself $O(h)$ (it is exactly $0$ under the
untilted Gaussian $\varphi$), so $\Cov_{\tilde q_h}(u,Q(u))=O(h)$, and the cross
term in \eqref{eq:expansion-terms} is $h^3\cdot O(h)=O(h^4)$, absorbed into the
stated $O(h^4)$. The final term $h^4\Cov_{\tilde q_h}[Q(u)]=O(h^4)$ trivially.
Combining, $\Cov_h[\mu(Y)]=h^2J(y)J(y)^\top+O(h^4)$, the between-group term of
\eqref{eq:expansion}.

Substituting Steps~2--3 into \eqref{eq:expansion-totcov} proves
\eqref{eq:expansion-pop}, i.e.\ the deterministic part of \eqref{eq:expansion}.

\emph{Step 4: Monte-Carlo fluctuation.} Write $S_N:=\frac1N\sum_jK_h(y-y^j)$ and
$T_N:=\frac1N\sum_jK_h(y-y^j)x^j$, so $\bar x_h(y)=T_N/S_N$. Each is an average of
$N$ i.i.d.\ terms; a standard kernel-density computation gives
$\Var\big(K_h(y-Y)\big)=\Theta(h^{-k})$ (the kernel has height $\Theta(h^{-k})$ on an
effective support of Lebesgue measure $\Theta(h^k)$, so its second moment is
$\Theta(h^{-k})\cdot\Theta(h^k)\cdot\Theta(h^{-k})=\Theta(h^{-k})$, dominating the
squared mean $\Theta(1)$), and likewise for $K_h(y-Y)X$. Hence
$S_N=\E[K_h(y-Y)]+O_P\big(\sqrt{h^{-k}/N}\big)$ and
$T_N=\E[K_h(y-Y)X]+O_P\big(\sqrt{h^{-k}/N}\big)$, i.e.\ each has \emph{relative}
fluctuation $O_P\big((Nh^k)^{-1/2}\big)$ around a mean of order $\Theta(1)$. A
first-order Taylor (Slutsky) expansion of the ratio $T_N/S_N$ around the population
ratio $\E_h[X]$ then gives $\bar x_h(y)=\E_h[X]+O_P\big((Nh^k)^{-1/2}\big)$, exactly
as in standard Nadaraya--Watson consistency theory; the same argument applied to the
(also ratio-of-averages) quadratic functional $\Covh(y)$ gives
$\Covh(y)=\Cov_h^{\mathrm{pop}}(y)+O_P\big((Nh^k)^{-1/2}\big)$, which is the
remaining term of \eqref{eq:expansion} and completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Nadaraya--Watson rates for the conditional mean]
\label{prop:nwrate}
Under the assumptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:expansion}, $\bar x_h(y)$ is exactly
the Nadaraya--Watson estimator of $\mu(y)$, with
\[
\mathrm{Bias}=O(h^2),\qquad \Var=O\big((Nh^k)^{-1}\big),\qquad
\mathrm{MSE}=O(h^4)+O\big((Nh^k)^{-1}\big),
\]
minimised at $h^\star\asymp N^{-1/(k+4)}$. Queries at $y=y^i$ are \emph{design
points}: $x^i$ receives the maximal weight $K_h(0)$, and $Nh^k\to\infty$ is exactly
what makes its relative influence vanish; without it the result does not apply there.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:tgtnoise}]
Fix $(x,t,y)$ with $t<1$ and condition on $I=i$. Then
$X_t=(1-t)X_0+t x^i+t\rho\xi$, so with $r:=x-tx^i$ we have
$r=(1-t)X_0+t\rho\xi$, a linear combination of the independent standard Gaussians
$X_0,\xi$; hence $r\sim\N(0,s_t^2I_d)$ with $s_t^2=(1-t)^2+t^2\rho^2$, and
$p(x\mid I=i,t)=s_t^{-d}\varphi_1(r/s_t)=\varphi_{s_t}(x-tx^i)$. As in
Proposition~\ref{prop:kernel-field}, given $I=i$ the interpolant depends only on
$(X_0,\xi)$ and $\widetilde Y=y^i+h\varepsilon$ only on $\varepsilon$, so
$X_t\perp\widetilde Y\mid I=i$ and Bayes' rule gives
$\Pr(I=i\mid X_t=x,t,\widetilde Y=y)\propto\varphi_{s_t}(x-tx^i)K_h(y-y^i)=w_i$, the
$i$-independent factor $1/N$ cancelling as before.

For the per-index target, $(X_0,\xi,r)$ are jointly Gaussian with
$\Cov(X_0,r)=(1-t)I_d$ and $\Cov(\xi,r)=t\rho I_d$, so
$\E[X_0\mid r]=\frac{1-t}{s_t^2}r$ and $\E[\xi\mid r]=\frac{t\rho}{s_t^2}r$. With
$U=X_1-X_0=x^i+\rho\xi-X_0$,
\[
\E[U\mid X_t=x,t,I=i]=x^i+\rho\,\tfrac{t\rho}{s_t^2}r-\tfrac{1-t}{s_t^2}r
=x^i+\tfrac{t\rho^2-(1-t)}{s_t^2}\,(x-tx^i),
\]
and averaging over the index posterior $w_i$ gives, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:projection},
\begin{equation}
\vstar(x,t,y)=\sum_i w_i(x,t,y)\Big[x^i+\tfrac{t\rho^2-(1-t)}{s_t^2}\,(x-tx^i)\Big],
\qquad w_i\propto\varphi_{s_t}(x-tx^i)\,K_h(y-y^i),
\label{eq:tgtfield}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi_s$ the $\N(0,s^2I_d)$ density.

For the endpoint law, argue exactly as in Lemma~\ref{lem:mixture-coupling} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}: consider the coupling $I\sim p^{(h)}(y)$,
$X_0\sim\pi_0$, $\xi\sim\N(0,I_d)$ mutually independent, $X_1:=x^I+\rho\xi$, with the
same linear interpolant. Its index posterior at $(x,t)$ is
$\propto p_i^{(h)}(y)\varphi_{s_t}(x-tx^i)$, the same proportionality in $i$ as
$w_i$, so its conditional velocity is $\vstar$; the weights are smooth and positive
and sum to one, so Lemma~\ref{lem:wellposed} applies and the flow transports $\pi_0$
to $\mathrm{Law}(X_t)$ for every $t<1$. Since $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1\to X_1$ almost
surely as $t\uparrow1$, $\mathrm{Law}(X_t)\Rightarrow\mathrm{Law}(x^I+\rho\xi)
=\sum_ip_i^{(h)}(y)\N(x^i,\rho^2I_d)$, which is \eqref{eq:tgtendpoint}. Its mean is
$\sum_ip_i^{(h)}x^i=\bar x_h(y)$ and, by the law of total covariance applied to the
mixture, its covariance is $\Covh(y)+\rho^2I_d$. Absolute continuity is immediate
since each component is; \eqref{eq:w2floor} was derived from
$\operatorname{supp}\subseteq\{x^i\}$ and so does not apply. Finally
$s_1^2=\rho^2>0$, so $t\mapsto(t\rho^2-(1-t))/s_t^2$ is continuous at $t=1$ (with
value $1$) and $\vstar$ is bounded on bounded sets up to and including $t=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:nwrate}]
The first two displays are the classical pointwise bias and variance of the
Nadaraya--Watson estimator at an interior point, and we claim no novelty for them; we
record here only that the hypotheses under which they are usually stated are exactly
the ones already imposed in Proposition~\ref{prop:expansion}, so nothing further is
assumed. Those hypotheses are: $(x^j,y^j)$ i.i.d.; $y$ interior to
$\operatorname{supp}(\rho_Y)$ with $\rho_Y(y)>0$ and $\rho_Y\in C^2$ near $y$;
$\mu\in C^2$ near $y$; $K$ a bounded, symmetric, compactly supported (or
sufficiently rapidly decaying) kernel with $\int K=1$ and finite second moment; and
the bandwidth regime $h\to0$, $Nh^k\to\infty$. Under precisely these,
$\mathrm{Bias}=\tfrac{h^2}{2}\mu_2(K)\big[\Delta\mu(y)
+2\nabla\mu(y)^\top\nabla\rho_Y(y)/\rho_Y(y)\big]+o(h^2)$ and
$\Var=\tfrac{R(K)}{Nh^k}\cdot\tfrac{\Sigma(y)}{\rho_Y(y)}+o\big((Nh^k)^{-1}\big)$,
where $\mu_2(K)=\int u_1^2K(u)\,du$ and $R(K)=\int K^2$; see
\citet[\S1.5 and \S1.6]{tsybakov2009nonparametric} or
\citet[Ch.~5]{gyorfi2002distribution}. The leading bias constant is the one obtained
by Taylor-expanding $\mu(y^j)$ about $y$ against the symmetric kernel weights, which
is the same computation as the $O(h^2)$ term in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:expansion}.

Only the last step is ours, and it is elementary. Writing
$\mathrm{MSE}(h)=c_1h^4+c_2(Nh^k)^{-1}+o(\cdot)$ with $c_1,c_2>0$ the squared-bias and
variance constants above, $\frac{d}{dh}\mathrm{MSE}=4c_1h^3-c_2kN^{-1}h^{-k-1}$
vanishes when $h^{k+4}=c_2k/(4c_1N)$, i.e.\ $h^\star\asymp N^{-1/(k+4)}$; the second
derivative is positive there, so it is the minimiser.

The design-point remark needs no separate argument: at $y=y^i$ the summand $j=i$
carries weight $K_h(0)/\sum_jK_h(y^i-y^j)$, and $\sum_{j}K_h(y^i-y^j)\asymp Nh^k$ in
probability under the stated regime, so that weight is $\Theta((Nh^k)^{-1})\to0$. When
$Nh^k\not\to\infty$ it does not vanish and the expansion above does not apply --- which
is exactly the fixed-$N$, $h\to0$ regime of Corollary~\ref{cor:zerobw}, where the
weight tends to $1$ instead.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:prop17}]
Given $I=i$, write $r:=x-tx^i$. Since $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tx^i+\gamma(t)Z$,
$r=(1-t)X_0+\gamma(t)Z$, a linear combination of the independent standard
Gaussians $X_0,Z\sim\N(0,I_d)$, so $r\sim\N(0,s_t^2I_d)$ with
$s_t^2=(1-t)^2+\gamma(t)^2$. For jointly Gaussian $(X_0,r)$,
$\Cov(X_0,r)=(1-t)I_d$ (the $\gamma(t)Z$ term is independent of $X_0$), so the
standard Gaussian conditional-mean formula gives
$\E[X_0\mid r]=\Cov(X_0,r)\,\Var(r)^{-1}r=\tfrac{1-t}{s_t^2}r$; identically,
$\Cov(Z,r)=\gamma(t)I_d$ gives $\E[Z\mid r]=\tfrac{\gamma(t)}{s_t^2}r$. Hence
\[
v_\sigma^\star(x,t,y^i)=\E[\dot X_t\mid X_t=x,t,I=i]
=x^i-\E[X_0\mid r]+\dot\gamma(t)\,\E[Z\mid r]
=x^i+\frac{-(1-t)+\dot\gamma(t)\gamma(t)}{s_t^2}\,r.
\]
Since $\gamma(t)^2=\sigma^2t(1-t)$, differentiating gives
$2\dot\gamma\gamma=\sigma^2(1-2t)$, i.e.\ $\dot\gamma\gamma=\tfrac{\sigma^2}2(1-2t)$,
which yields (a)'s expression for $c(t)$. For the logarithmic-derivative identity,
$\frac{d}{dt}s_t^2=-2(1-t)+2\dot\gamma\gamma=2s_t^2c(t)$ by the same computation, so
$c(t)=\tfrac12\frac{d}{dt}\log s_t^2$.

(b) Along the flow, $u_t:=x_t-tx^i$ satisfies $\dot u_t=\dot x_t-x^i
=v_\sigma^\star(x_t,t,y^i)-x^i=c(t)u_t$ by (a) (with $r=u_t$ evaluated on the
trajectory), a linear ODE with solution
$u_t=u_0\exp\!\big(\int_0^tc(\tau)\,d\tau\big)
=x_0\exp\!\big(\tfrac12[\log s_t^2-\log s_0^2]\big)=x_0\,s_t$, using $s_0=1$ and
$u_0=x_0-0\cdot x^i=x_0$. Hence $x_t=tx^i+s_tx_0$.

(c) Since $s_1=(1-1)[(1-1)+\sigma^2\cdot1]=0$, (b) gives $x_1=x^i$ for every
$x_0\sim\pi_0$ and every $\sigma\ge0$, so $p_1^{\mathrm{cond}}(\cdot\mid y^i)=\delta_{x^i}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[What interpolant noise does and does not change]
\label{cor:prop18}
Interpolant noise does not change the population endpoint law
\eqref{eq:invariance} for any $\sigma$; it \emph{does} raise the irreducible error
($\inf_v L_\sigma>0$ for $\sigma>0$, whereas $\inf_v L_0=0$) and halves the Lipschitz
blow-up rate: $\lim_{t\uparrow1}(1-t)|c(t)|=1$ for $\sigma=0$ and $\tfrac12$ for
$\sigma>0$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:prop18}]
\emph{Endpoint invariance} is Proposition~\ref{prop:prop17}(c).

\emph{Irreducible error.} For $\sigma=0$, $\gamma\equiv0$ so $\dot X_t=X_1-X_0$ is
exactly the target of Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}, giving $\inf_vL_0=0$. For
$\sigma>0$: by the same projection argument as Proposition~\ref{prop:projection},
applied to $\dot X_t$ and $(X_t,t,Y)$, it suffices to show
$\Var(\dot X_t\mid X_t=x,t,I=i)>0$ for some positive-measure set of $t$. Writing
$\dot X_t-x^i=-X_0+\dot\gamma(t)Z$ and $r=(1-t)X_0+\gamma(t)Z$ as in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:prop17}, the standard Gaussian conditioning formula gives,
for $u=(-1,\dot\gamma(t))$ and $w=(1-t,\gamma(t))$,
\[
\Var(\dot X_t\mid r,I=i)=|u|^2-\frac{(u\cdot w)^2}{|w|^2}
=\big(1+\dot\gamma(t)^2\big)-c(t)^2s_t^2,
\]
using $u\cdot w=-(1-t)+\dot\gamma(t)\gamma(t)=c(t)s_t^2$ from the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:prop17}. This vanishes only if $u\parallel w$, i.e.\
$\dot\gamma(t)(1-t)=-\gamma(t)$; substituting
$\gamma(t)=\sigma\sqrt{t(1-t)}$ and $\dot\gamma(t)=\sigma(1-2t)/(2\sqrt{t(1-t)})$
reduces this to $1-2t=-2t$, i.e.\ $1=0$---impossible. So for $\sigma>0$ the
residual variance is strictly positive for \emph{every} $t\in(0,1)$, and
$\inf_vL_\sigma(v)=\E[\Var(\dot X_t\mid X_t,t,Y)]>0$.

\emph{Lipschitz rate.} From (a),
$(1-t)c(t)=\dfrac{-(1-t)+\frac{\sigma^2}2(1-2t)}{(1-t)+\sigma^2t}$, which as
$t\uparrow1$ tends to $-1$ if $\sigma=0$ and to $-\sigma^2/2\;/\;\sigma^2=-\tfrac12$
if $\sigma>0$, giving the stated limits.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:factors}]
By Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint}, for $h>0$ (and $\sigma=0$) the endpoint law
$p_h^{\mathrm{gen}}(\cdot\mid y)=\sum_ip_i^{(h)}(y)\delta_{x^i}$ depends on $h$
only through the label factor $K_h(y-y^i)$ in \eqref{eq:kernel-field}, and
Corollaries~\ref{cor:zerobw}--\ref{cor:infbw} show it genuinely interpolates
between two distinct endpoint laws as $h$ ranges over $[0,\infty)$. By contrast,
for $h=0$ with distinct labels and any $\sigma\ge0$, $Y=y^i$ already pins $I=i$
deterministically, so no index-posterior argument is needed: Proposition~\ref{prop:prop17}
shows directly that although the spatial factor of Lemma~\ref{lem:cov},
$\N(tx^i,(1-t)^2I_d)$, is replaced by $\N(tx^i,s_t^2I_d)$ for \emph{every} $i$
identically, the endpoint is exactly invariant, $p_1^{\mathrm{cond}}(\cdot\mid y^i)
=\delta_{x^i}$ for every $\sigma$. Since perturbing the label factor changes $p_1$
while perturbing the spatial factor identically across $i$ does not, the two
mechanisms act through different, non-interchangeable channels.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:liplb}]
Let $X,X'$ be i.i.d.\ copies of $X_t\mid I=i\sim\N(tx^i,(1-t)^2I_d)$ and
$e(\cdot)=f_i(\cdot,t)-v(\cdot,t,y^i)$. Since
$f_i(X,t)-f_i(X',t)=-(X-X')/(1-t)$ and $X-X'\sim\N(0,2(1-t)^2I_d)$,
\[
\E|f_i(X,t)-f_i(X',t)|^2=\frac{2(1-t)^2d}{(1-t)^2}=2d.
\]
By $L$-Lipschitzness, $\E|v(X,t,y^i)-v(X',t,y^i)|^2\le L^2\E|X-X'|^2=2dL^2(1-t)^2$.
Also $\E|e(X)-e(X')|^2=2\E|e(X)|^2-2|\E e(X)|^2\le2\E|e(X)|^2$ (since $X,X'$ are
i.i.d.). Applying the $L^2(\Omega)$ triangle inequality to
$f_i(X,t)-f_i(X',t)=[e(X)-e(X')]+[v(X,t,y^i)-v(X',t,y^i)]$,
\[
\sqrt{2d}\ \le\ \sqrt{2\,\E|e(X)|^2}\ +\ \sqrt{2d}\,L(1-t),
\]
hence $\sqrt{\E|e(X)|^2}\ge\sqrt d\,[1-L(1-t)]_+$, i.e.\ $\E|e(X)|^2\ge d[1-L(1-t)]_+^2$,
which is \eqref{eq:floor}'s pointwise bound with $e(X)=v(X_t,t,y^i)-f_i(X_t,t)$ up to
sign. (Using the sharp identity $\E|e(X)-e(X')|^2=2\E|e(X)|^2-2|\E e(X)|^2\le2\E|e(X)|^2$,
rather than the cruder bound $4\E|e(X)|^2$, is what preserves the constant $d$ in
\eqref{eq:floor}; a weaker triangle-inequality step would give only a smaller floor.)
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}]
Substituting $s=1-t$, $\int_0^{1/L}(1-Ls)^2\,ds=\big[s-Ls^2+\tfrac{L^2s^3}3\big]_0^{1/L}
=\tfrac1L-\tfrac1L+\tfrac1{3L}=\tfrac1{3L}$. Proposition~\ref{prop:liplb}'s bound
\eqref{eq:floor}'s integrand holds for every training index $i$, hence for every
$v\in\mathcal F_L$ and every $t\in[1-1/L,1)$, so it survives averaging over
$I\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\dots,N\}$ and $t\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, giving
$\inf_{v\in\mathcal F_L}L_0(v)\ge d\int_{1-1/L}^1[1-L(1-t)]^2\,dt=d/(3L)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Finite-sample memorisation and exact non-representability}
\label{sec:theory-d2}

This subsection translates two representability results from the unconditional
gradient-variance/stochastic-interpolant literature \citep{gradvar2025} into the
present notation: (i)~why a fixed finite \emph{unconditional} batch already admits a
zero-loss memorising field despite Proposition~\ref{prop:uncond}'s positive
population loss, and (ii)~why no finite network can exactly represent the collapsed
field $(\star)$. Unlike Sections~\ref{sec:theory-hard}--\ref{sec:theory-floor}, the
finite sample itself is treated as random here.

\begin{lemma}[Generic non-intersection of finite interpolant segments]
\label{lem:nonintersect}
Let $x_0^1,\dots,x_0^N\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\pi_0$ and, independently,
$z^1,\dots,z^N\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\rho_1$ be $N$ unconditional
source--target pairs (no labels), both laws absolutely continuous on $\R^d$, and
$\ell_i(t)=(1-t)x_0^i+tz^i$, $t\in[0,1]$.
(a) If $d\ge2$: for any fixed $i\ne j$,
$\Pr[\exists\,t\in(0,1):\ell_i(t)=\ell_j(t)]=0$.
(b) If $d>2$: for any fixed $i\ne j$,
$\Pr[\exists\,t_i,t_j\in(0,1),\,t_i\ne t_j:\ell_i(t_i)=\ell_j(t_j)]=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) For a \emph{fixed} $\hat t\in(0,1)$, $\ell_i(\hat t)=\ell_j(\hat t)$ solves to
$z^j=z^i+\tfrac{1-\hat t}{\hat t}(x_0^i-x_0^j)$, which pins $z^j$ to a single
\emph{point}, not to a subspace; and that point moves with $\hat t$, so a fixed-$\hat t$
argument does not by itself control the event ``$\exists t$''. Take the union
directly instead. Conditionally on $(x_0^i,x_0^j,z^i)$,
\[
\{\exists\,t\in(0,1):\ell_i(t)=\ell_j(t)\}
=\Big\{z^j\in R\Big\},\qquad
R:=\big\{z^i+s\,(x_0^i-x_0^j)\ :\ s\in(0,\infty)\big\},
\]
since $s=(1-t)/t$ is a bijection from $(0,1)$ onto $(0,\infty)$. $R$ is a half-line,
hence contained in a one-dimensional affine subspace of $\R^d$ and Lebesgue-null for
$d\ge2$ (if $x_0^i=x_0^j$, which happens with probability $0$, $R$ is the single point
$z^i$, still null). As $\rho_1$ is absolutely continuous and $z^j$ is independent of
$(x_0^i,x_0^j,z^i)$, the conditional probability is $0$, and integrating preserves it.
A union bound over the finitely many pairs $(i,j)$ finishes (a). (b) With $t_i\ne t_j$
both free the same union argument gives
$z^j\in\{[(1-t_i)x_0^i+t_iz^i-(1-t_j)x_0^j]/t_j:(t_i,t_j)\in(0,1)^2\}$, the image of a
two-dimensional parameter set under a map that is smooth on compact subsets, hence a
countable union of Lipschitz images of bounded planar sets; each has Hausdorff dimension
at most $2$ and so is Lebesgue-null when $d>2$. The rest is identical.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A memorising field always exists on a fixed finite unconditional batch]
\label{prop:memorize}
Fix $N$ unconditional pairs $\{(x_0^i,z^i)\}_{i=1}^N$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:nonintersect}
with $d>2$ and the $z^i$ pairwise distinct a.s., and sample $m$ time points
$t^{(i,j)}\in(0,1)$, $j=1,\dots,m$, independently across $(i,j)$. Let
$X_t^{(i,j)}=(1-t^{(i,j)})x_0^i+t^{(i,j)}z^i$ and
\[
L_{\mathrm{MC}}^{\mathrm{unc}}(v)=\frac1{Nm}\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^m
\big|(z^i-x_0^i)-v\big(X_t^{(i,j)},t^{(i,j)}\big)\big|^2.
\]
Then, almost surely over the draw of $\{(x_0^i,z^i)\}$ and $\{t^{(i,j)}\}$, some
$v$ attains $L_{\mathrm{MC}}^{\mathrm{unc}}(v)=0$---even though
$\inf_v L_{\mathrm{unc}}(v)>0$ at the population level whenever the $z^i$ are not
all equal (Proposition~\ref{prop:uncond}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:nonintersect}(a)--(b), union-bounded over the $\binom N2$ pairs,
almost surely no two of the $N$ full segments $\{\ell_i(t):t\in(0,1)\}$ intersect at
all, so in particular the $Nm$ sampled points $(X_t^{(i,j)},t^{(i,j)})$ never
collide across different $i$. On this full-measure event $(z,t)\mapsto i$ is well
defined on the sample, so setting
$v(z,t):=z^{i(z,t)}-x_0^{i(z,t)}$ on the sample and $v:=0$ elsewhere gives
$v(X_t^{(i,j)},t^{(i,j)})=z^i-x_0^i$ exactly for every sampled $(i,j)$, so every
summand of $L_{\mathrm{MC}}^{\mathrm{unc}}$ vanishes.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why SGD does not land here]
\label{rem:resample}
Proposition~\ref{prop:memorize} is valid only for one \emph{fixed} finite sample.
Standard CFM training resamples $x_0^i$ (and the time samples) independently every
epoch, so the lookup table fit at epoch $k$ is checked, at epoch $k+1$, against
points it never saw; no single lookup table stays consistent with a growing stream
of $(x_0,t)$ draws. This is the precise mechanism behind the informal claim that
resampling $x_0$---not mere continuity of $\pi_0$---is what pushes unconditional
training toward the population optimum of Proposition~\ref{prop:uncond} rather than
a per-example memorising solution, and it gives a concrete reading of P6
(Table~\ref{tab:sweeps}): at fixed capacity, larger $N$ makes the epoch-consistent
lookup table harder to realise with a smooth finite-parameter function, pushing the
attained solution back toward the population optimum. Note the asymmetry with
Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}: conditional collapse needs no genericity argument
and no fixed-batch caveat, because $y^i$ pins down $i$ deterministically for every
$x_0$, not merely with probability $1$ on one finite draw---resampling $x_0$ every
epoch does nothing to prevent it, which is exactly why conditional collapse is
universal while unconditional memorisation is not.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[The collapse field cannot be exactly represented by a finite network]
\label{lem:norepr}
Let $g(t)=1/(1-t)$ on $[0,1)$ and let $f_\theta:\R\to\R$ be any finite-depth,
finite-width feedforward network with finite real weights, built from affine layers
and globally Lipschitz activations (this covers ReLU, leaky-ReLU, tanh, sigmoid,
GELU/SiLU, softplus, and every activation used in our experiments). Then
$\sup_{t\in[0,1)}|f_\theta(t)-g(t)|=\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A finite composition of affine maps (finite weight matrices, hence finite operator
norm) and globally Lipschitz activations is globally Lipschitz on $\R$, with
constant equal to the product of the finitely many layer constants. A Lipschitz
function is bounded on any bounded set: fixing $t_0\in[0,1)$,
$|f_\theta(t)|\le|f_\theta(t_0)|+K|t-t_0|\le|f_\theta(t_0)|+K$ for all $t\in[0,1)$,
so $\sup_{t\in[0,1)}|f_\theta(t)|<\infty$. But $g(t)\to\infty$ as $t\uparrow1$, so
$g$ is unbounded on $[0,1)$, hence so is $f_\theta-g$.
\end{proof}

For $x\ne x^i$ fixed, $t\mapsto v_0^\star(x,t,y^i)=(x^i-x)/(1-t)$ has exactly this
$1/(1-t)$ blow-up along the direction $x^i-x$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:norepr} (applied
coordinatewise to that direction) shows that \emph{no} finite network of the kind
used in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} can represent $v_0^\star$ \emph{exactly} on
all of $[0,1)$: for every fixed parameter vector $\theta$ there is some $(x,t)$ at
which $v_\theta\ne v_0^\star$. This does not contradict universal approximation,
which guarantees only \emph{uniform} approximation on \emph{compact} sets: every
$[0,1-\delta]$, $\delta>0$, is disjoint from the singularity at $t=1$---exactly the
regime the sampler must traverse to reach the collapse point $x^i$, and exactly
where the ODE solver's $t=1$ handling matters in practice. We stop short, however,
of claiming this pointwise non-representability forces
$L^\star_{\mathrm{model}}-L^\star_{\mathrm{all}}>0$ for an architecture whose
weights (hence Lipschitz constant) are unconstrained: pointwise blow-up on a
shrinking neighbourhood of $t=1$ is compatible, in principle, with a sequence of
increasingly ill-conditioned finite networks driving the \emph{expected} loss
$L_0(v_\theta)$ arbitrarily close to $0$ (consistent with the growing empirical
Lipschitz constant we measure in Figure~\ref{fig:lipschitz}). The genuine,
quantitative loss floor we prove, $d/(3L)$, therefore applies only to the
Lipschitz-capped class $\mathcal F_L$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}, and should not
be read as a statement about arbitrary finite architectures.

\subsection{Architecture and optimiser sensitivity}

Every headline result in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} uses one fixed architecture
(4-layer, width-128 MLP, Adam). Since Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}
constrains only the \emph{population} minimiser, not any parameterisation, the
theory predicts collapse for \emph{any} sufficiently expressive architecture; what
it does not predict is how fast a given architecture and optimiser reach it within
a fixed iteration budget. We re-ran EXP-1 (conditional, $2$ seeds each) varying one
factor at a time---width $\in\{32,64,256\}$ at depth $4$, depth $\in\{2,6\}$ at

toward the same population optimum) together with the optimisation-gap caveat of
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} (how close a given budget gets you depends on the
architecture and optimiser). We did not extend this sweep to the Lipschitz
diagnostic of Appendix~\ref{sec:aux} or to EXP-2/EXP-3; doing so is left to future

\subsection{Adversarial pairing: collapse does not require a real posterior}
\label{sec:adversarial}

Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} never uses $A$ or $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}$: it needs
only that $y^1,\dots,y^N$ be pairwise distinct. It therefore predicts that collapse
is unchanged if $Y$ is \emph{randomly permuted relative to $X$} before training, so
that $(x^i,y^i)$ is no longer the true generative pair---identification, not
discovered posterior structure. This mirrors the ``Adversarial Pairings'' experiment
of \citet{gradvar2025} (shuffled OT-coupling targets on CelebA, unconditional),
adapted to the conditional case where the prediction is exact.
Re-running EXP-1 unchanged (5 seeds), permuting $Y$ independently per seed,
$\tr\Cov$ collapses under shuffled labels just as under the real pairing, from
$\gtrsim1$ to $0.625\pm0.108$ at $2\times10^5$ (vs.\ $0.396\pm0.127$ real); the gap
is optimisation difficulty at a fixed budget---a random $x$--$y$ pairing is a harder
target to fit---not a difference in the population optimum, which
Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} fixes at $0$ either way. The sharper diagnostic is
$\|\text{mean}-\mu_{\mathrm{post}}(y^i)\|$, the distance to $y^i$'s \emph{true}
analytic posterior mean (still computable from $A$, but now unrelated to the $x^i$
the label was shuffled onto): it does not shrink but \emph{grows} throughout
training, $0.84\pm0.13\to1.437\pm0.176$, crossing above
$\|\text{mean}-x^i\|$ ($1.35\pm0.10\to0.559\pm0.104$) between $10^4$ and
$3\times10^4$ iterations and staying above it thereafter
(Figures~\ref{fig:adv-trace}--\ref{fig:adv-mean}, appendix). The generated law
tracks the arbitrary label-to-example assignment, not any statistical relationship
between $x$ and $y$---Corollary~\ref{cor:distinction}'s conclusion, confirmed even
when the labels carry no real information about $x$ at all.

\subsection{The same exposure control on EXP-1 (empirical)}
\label{sec:p6exposure}

\subsection{What the EXP-1 error bars are measuring (empirical)}
\label{sec:seedsplit}

Section~\ref{sec:experiments} notes that \texttt{seed} drives both the problem instance
(the operator $A$, hence $\Sigmapost$, and the dataset draw) and all training
randomness, so every $\pm$ in this paper mixes the two. A separate
\texttt{problem\_seed} makes them independent. Two sweeps of ten runs each at $2\times
10^5$ iterations---one holding the instance fixed and varying the run, one the
reverse---give the split.

The problem instance carries $0.1261^2/(0.1261^2+0.0675^2)=78\%$ of the variance and the

We report it rather than retrofitting it into the existing tables, whose numbers were
produced under the conflated protocol.

\subsection{An $N$-sweep on EXP-3, and what it actually measures (empirical)}
\label{sec:nsweep3}

Table~\ref{tab:sweeps} sweeps $N$ on EXP-1 at a fixed iteration budget. We repeat
that sweep on the image experiment, and add the control the EXP-1 version lacks.

At $N\in\{100,500,2000\}$ and a fixed $30000$ iterations with batch $64$
(3 seeds each), the inpaint-region pixel variance rises monotonically with $N$, by
$14.9\times$, with a log--log slope of $+0.90$ (Table~\ref{tab:nsweep3}A). Taken
alone that looks like a clean scaling law in $N$. It is not safe to read it that
way. At a fixed budget the number of gradient samples each training image receives
is $30000\times64/N$, i.e.\ exactly proportional to $1/N$, so ``variance grows like
$N$'' and ``variance grows as optimisation progress per image shrinks'' are the same
curve. A slope of $\approx+1$ is what \emph{either} explanation predicts.

Two controls separate them. Holding the per-image exposure fixed at $3840$ gradient
samples instead ($N{=}100$ at $6$k iterations, $N{=}500$ at $30$k, $N{=}2000$ at
$120$k), the slope drops to $-0.29$ and the spread from $14.9\times$ to
$2.3\times$; $N{=}500$ and $N{=}2000$ then differ by $3\%$, against a $17\%$
seed-to-seed standard deviation (Table~\ref{tab:nsweep3}B, Figure~\ref{fig:nsweep3}).
Lining the runs up by training loss instead of by iteration---a control that does not
assume exposure is the right currency---gives the same answer: $N{=}500$ and
$N{=}2000$ agree to $6$--$13\%$ with inconsistent sign at every loss level, while
$N{=}100$ sits about $2\times$ lower (Table~\ref{tab:nsweep3}C).

So most of the apparent $N$-dependence at fixed budget is an optimisation-budget
effect, and the residual genuine effect is a factor $\approx2$ rather than
$\approx15$. This is what Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} would lead one to expect:
the population minimiser is collapsed for \emph{every} finite $N$, so a large
intrinsic $N$-dependence would be the surprising outcome. It also sharpens the
caveat already attached to the EXP-1 sweep in Table~\ref{tab:sweeps}, which shares
the same fixed-budget design and therefore the same confound: that sweep is best
read as a statement about how far a fixed budget gets you at each $N$, not about
how the population optimum depends on $N$. Appendix~\ref{sec:p6exposure} reruns the
EXP-1 sweep under exactly this control and finds the same thing there
($20.2\times\to2.28\times$), on a different architecture, dimension and dataset.

\subsection{Additional verification (gap to optimum, Lipschitz, posterior distance)}
\label{sec:aux}

Three checkpoint-only measurements corroborate the theory.
(i)~\emph{Optimality gap.} The Monte-Carlo gap $L(v_\theta)-L(\vstar)$, with
$L(\vstar)=\E[\Var(U\mid X_t,t,\widetilde Y)]$ computed from the exact minimiser, is
non-negative at every checkpoint and decreases monotonically toward $0$ at every $h$
(e.g.\ $h{=}0.1$: $1.14\to0.22\to0.102$ at $10^2/3{\times}10^4/2{\times}10^5$ steps),
consistent with ratio-to-kernel $\to1$. The irreducible error $L(\vstar)$ increases
with $h$ ($0\to1.88$): the price of smoothing (Figure~\ref{fig:optgap}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig_optgap.png}
\caption{Optimality gap $L(v_\theta)-L(v_h^\star)$ decays monotonically \emph{toward}
$0$ at every $h$ (left)---it is still positive at the last checkpoint
($0.09$--$0.44$), which is the optimisation gap of
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}; smoothing raises the irreducible error
$L(v_h^\star)$ (right).}
\label{fig:optgap}
\end{figure}

(ii)~\emph{Lipschitz floor.} The empirical $\operatorname{Lip}_x v_\theta$ grows from
$7.1$ at $t{=}0.5$ to $65.7$ at $t{=}0.99$; the Corollary~\ref{cor:floor} floor
$d/(3L)\approx0.010$ is $\sim50\times$ below the observed plateau $0.48$, so this
particular representation-capacity argument does \emph{not} explain the plateau: the
measured plateau is inconsistent with the Lipschitz-floor explanation tested here (we
have ruled out \emph{this} mechanism, not every possible capacity/representation
effect) and, as the learning-rate-schedule result of Section~\ref{sec:exp1} shows, is
strongly schedule-sensitive---suggesting an optimisation rather than a Lipschitz-capacity
limitation, though we do not have a positive quantitative theory of the optimisation
gap itself (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) (Figure~\ref{fig:lipschitz}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig_lipschitz.png}
\caption{The empirical Lipschitz constant of $v_\theta$ blows up as $t\to1$ (left); the
representation floor $d/(3L)$ is $\sim50\times$ below the observed loss plateau (right),
ruling out this particular Lipschitz-capacity explanation and pointing toward an
optimisation-limited plateau instead.}
\label{fig:lipschitz}
\end{figure}

(iii)~\emph{Posterior distance.} The MMD to the analytic posterior $\N(\mu_{\mathrm{post}},
\Sigmapost)$ falls with $h$ ($0.256\to0.012$) but never reaches $0$, and the Sinkhorn
distance plateaus near $2.7$ (Figure~\ref{fig:postdist}). An earlier version of this
paper read that plateau as a numerical witness of the floor \eqref{eq:w2floor}. That
reading is wrong, and Section~\ref{sec:p7} now reports the correction: the floor is
directly computable, equals $0.0353$ here---$3.5\%$ of $\tr\Sigmapost$---and the
population-optimal atomic law already reaches ${\rm MMD}=0.0089$, well below what the
trained model attains. The residual MMD is therefore dominated by kernel bias and the
optimisation gap, not by atomicity. Atomicity is real (Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity}
is exact) but it is not what these two curves measure.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig_posterior_distance.png}
\caption{MMD (left) falls monotonically with $h$ but never reaches $0$; the Sinkhorn
distance (right) drops sharply from $h{=}0$ and then flattens near $2.7$ (it is not
monotone over $h\ge0.05$, varying only within $3.1$--$3.5$ there). Neither approaches
$0$ at any $h$---the $h$-independent atomicity floor of
Proposition~\ref{prop:atomicity}.}
\label{fig:postdist}
\end{figure}

(iv)~\emph{Does the gap close with budget?} Items (i)--(iii) leave open whether the
residual gap at $h>0$ is an optimisation gap that a longer run would close, or a
representation floor that no budget can. We test this directly by extending the
$h{=}0.1$ run to $10^6$ iterations under the cosine schedule of
Section~\ref{sec:exp1}, over 5 seeds (Table~\ref{tab:gaph01},
Figure~\ref{fig:gaph01}). The covariance reaches the kernel optimum early and stays
there ($\tr\Cov/\tr\Covh = 1.65 \to 1.00$ by $10^5$, then flat to $10^6$), while the
mean error $\|\text{mean}-\bar x_h\|$ keeps falling, $0.188 \to 0.0534$ at $10^5$
$\to 0.0220$ at $10^6$: a further factor $2.4$ over the last decade of budget, with
no sign of a floor. A representation limit would show as a plateau; none appears
within $10^6$ iterations. This is positive evidence for the optimisation-paced
reading of Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, complementing the elimination argument in
(ii)---though it bounds the gap from one side only, and does not tell us where the
floor eventually lies.

Read this against the \emph{wrong} lens for comparison. The relative velocity error
to the collapsed field $(x^{i}-x)/(1-t)$ of Section~\ref{sec:theory-hard} barely moves at $h{=}0.1$
($0.78\to0.65$ over the same $10^6$ iterations) while at $h{=}0$ it falls
$0.78\to0.15$. That is not a representation limit either: the reference $\vstar$ at
$h{=}0$ does not depend on $h$, so at $h{=}0.1$ a model that correctly refuses to
collapse \emph{must} stay far from it. The $h$-aware Proposition~\ref{prop:moments}
quantities are the only ones that answer the question, which is why they are what
Table~\ref{tab:gaph01} reports.
